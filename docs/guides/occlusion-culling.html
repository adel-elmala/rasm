Culling and the frame

Occlusion culling with Hi-Z

An object can be inside the camera's view and still be invisible, because something in front of it hides it. Occlusion culling finds those objects and skips them. This page explains the GPU way to do it: a pyramid of depth values (the Hi-Z), a cheap test of each object's bounds against it, and the two-pass trick that gets a depth buffer before the frame is drawn. The last part shows how RASM does it.

In short

  • An object is occluded when its nearest point is farther than the farthest thing already drawn over all the pixels it covers.
  • The Hi-Z (hierarchical depth) is a mip chain of the depth buffer where each texel keeps the farthest depth of the 2 × 2 texels below it. With reversed-Z, farthest means smallest.
  • Each object's bounding volume becomes a screen rectangle and a nearest depth. The test reads the level where the rectangle covers at most 2 × 2 texels: 4 reads per object.
  • Every rounding goes toward "visible". A wrong "visible" costs a draw; a wrong "occluded" makes an object vanish.
  • The depth must exist before the frame is drawn. The two-pass scheme draws what was visible last frame, builds the Hi-Z from that, then tests the rest.
  • A stale guess from last frame only costs time, never pixels: the second pass catches every object the first pass missed.

1The problem

Frustum culling removes what is outside the view. In a city, a building interior or a forest, most of what is inside the view is hidden behind something closer. The GPU still runs the vertex shader for those objects, rasterizes their triangles, and only then throws each pixel away in the depth test. Drawing pixels that are later covered, or that fail the depth test, is called overdraw.

Occlusion culling is the same idea as frustum culling, with a different test: is this whole object behind what is already in the depth buffer? And the same rule applies: it may keep an invisible object, but it must never remove a visible one.

The Hi-Z test fits GPU-driven rendering well: the culling shader that already does the frustum test adds a few lines, and the visible list goes straight into an indirect draw. The rest of this page is about it.

3The depth pyramid (Hi-Z)

The depth buffer has one value per pixel. Testing an object that covers 10,000 pixels would need 10,000 reads. The fix is a pyramid like a mip chain, where each level is half the size of the one before. A texel in a coarse level summarizes a square of pixels with one number: the farthest depth in that square.

Farthest means max or min

Depth conventionNearFarFarthest of 4Nearest of 4
Standard01maxmin
Reversed-Z (see projection and depth)10minmax

Getting this backwards is the classic Hi-Z bug: the pyramid then holds the nearest depth, and objects vanish whenever something small stands in front of part of them.

Odd sizes

A 1920 × 1080 screen halves to 960 × 540, then 480 × 270, then 240 × 135. Now 135 is odd. If the next level is 67 rows and each texel reads 2 rows, the last row of the 135 is read by nobody. Its depth may be the farthest one, so the level above is wrong in the unsafe direction.

Building it

Each level is a compute dispatch (or a full-screen draw) that reads the level before and writes the min or max of 2 × 2. Each level depends on the one before, so there is a barrier between levels: about 11 dispatches for a 1080p screen. Two common speed-ups:

  • Single-pass downsampling (AMD FidelityFX SPD): one dispatch builds up to 12 levels, with groups that finish last carrying on to the small levels.
  • Min/max samplers (Vulkan's sampler filter minmax, core since 1.2): a bilinear fetch returns the min or max of the 4 texels it touches, so one fetch replaces four reads.

4From a bounding volume to a screen rectangle

Each object has a bounding sphere or box (see frustum culling). For the test it is turned into two things: the rectangle of pixels it can cover, and its nearest depth, the depth of its closest point.

Way to get the rectangleCostTightness
Project the 8 corners of a box (around the sphere, or the object's own box)8 matrix multipliesA bit loose for spheres. Works for any projection, perspective or orthographic.
Exact bounds of a projected sphere (Mara and McGuire, 2013)a few square rootsThe tightest rectangle a sphere can have, for perspective cameras.
Center ± radius / depthvery cheapWrong near the screen's edges, where a projected sphere is an ellipse wider than that. Avoid.

When the volume crosses the near plane, or reaches behind the camera, the projection breaks down: points behind the eye flip to the other side of the screen. The only safe answer is "visible". Objects that close are almost always visible anyway.

5Picking the level

The test should read only a few texels. So it picks the finest level where the rectangle touches at most 2 × 2 texels. With a rectangle w × h pixels, a first guess is

level = ceil(log2(max(w, h)))

At that level a texel is at least as big as the rectangle, so the rectangle touches at most 2 texels in each direction, however it is placed. But the guess is sometimes one level too coarse. A better way is to check the real texel coordinates and go one level finer when they still fit:

A finer level is not only fewer reads: its texels cover less space around the object, so they are less likely to include a far background pixel that makes the test fail.

6The test, and staying conservative

Read the 2 × 2 texels, take the farthest of them, and compare it with the object's nearest depth:

Depth conventionFarthest of the 4 texelsOccluded when
Standardf = max(4 texels)nearest > f
Reversed-Zf = min(4 texels)nearest < f

In words: the object's closest point is farther away than the farthest thing drawn anywhere in that area. Every step on the way rounds in the same direction:

StepRounds toward "visible" because
Box around the spherebigger rectangle, nearer nearest depth

The price of all this rounding is false visibles: objects that are hidden but kept. That is a cost in time only.

7Where the depth comes from

Here is the catch. To test objects against the depth buffer before drawing them, the depth buffer must already exist, but it is made by drawing them. There are three ways around this.

WayHowProblem
Occluder prepassDraw a hand-picked set of big occluders first, build the Hi-Z from them.Someone must choose the occluders; small objects never occlude.
Last frame's depth, reprojectedMove last frame's depth into this frame's view, build the Hi-Z from it.Reprojection leaves holes where something was hidden last frame; moving objects make it wrong. It needs care to stay conservative.
Two-passDraw what was visible last frame, build the Hi-Z from that, then test everything else.Two culling passes and a Hi-Z build in the middle of the frame.

The two-pass scheme (Haar and Aaltonen, SIGGRAPH 2015) is the usual choice today. It keeps one flag per object: was it visible last frame?

Why the guess only costs time

Last frameThis frameWhat happensCost
visiblevisibledrawn earlynone: the common case
visiblehiddendrawn early anyway; the late test clears its flagone wasted draw, for one frame
hiddenvisibleskipped early; the late test sees it against this frame's depth and draws itnone for the image; it is drawn late
hiddenhiddenskipped early, culled latenone: this is the saving

The Hi-Z of the late pass holds only the early objects. It may lack some occluders, but missing occluders only make objects look more visible, never less. So the late test is still conservative. A new object, or a camera cut, starts with no good history: the early pass draws little, and the late pass does most of the work for one frame.

9Variants and pitfalls

Finer culling

Whole objects are coarse: a long wall with one visible corner is drawn whole. Engines that split meshes into small clusters (meshlets) of about 64 to 128 triangles run the same test per cluster, and some go down to single triangles.

Other views

Each camera needs its own Hi-Z and its own history. A shadow map's light view sees different occluders than the main camera.

  • Transparent and alpha-blended objects must not write the depth the Hi-Z is built from: they do not hide what is behind them.
  • History is per object and per view. If the flags of two cameras get mixed, the result is still correct, only slower.
  • Draw order can change. An object moves between the early and the late pass when its test flips. Two surfaces at exactly the same depth can then swap which one wins. Fix z-fighting in the content; do not rely on draw order.
  • Viewport and depth range. The test maps clip space onto the Hi-Z. If the pass draws into part of the target, or with another depth range, that mapping must include it.
  • Build cost. The Hi-Z build is a fixed cost per view. In a scene with little occlusion, it can cost more than it saves. Measure it with a GPU timer.

10In RASM

RASM uses the two-pass Hi-Z scheme from section 7, inside every drawScene call. It is on by default; EngineConfig::occlusionCulling turns it off. Both culling passes run the same two compute dispatches, countMain and writeMain in cull.slang (see stream compaction), with a phase in the push constant: CULL_EARLY or CULL_LATE. The frustum test runs first in both (frustum culling).

The Hi-Z in RASM

The test in RASM

isOccluded in occlusionCulling.slang follows sections 4 to 6 closely:

  1. Move the world-space sphere into view space and take the 8 corners of the box around it. Project each one.
  2. A corner with clip w ≤ 0 (behind the camera), or a nearest depth ≥ 1 (reaching the near plane), means visible.
  3. The rectangle is clamped to the viewport in pixels. Its level-0 texels are the pixels shifted right by 1.
  4. Go up one level at a time while the texel range is wider than 2 in x or y. The 1 × 1 top level always fits.
  5. Read the 4 corner texels of the range, take the min (the farthest), and return nearestDepth < farthest.

Pausing the pass

The Hi-Z needs the early draws' depth, but those draws are inside a render pass, where Vulkan allows no dispatches. So Engine::recordOcclusionCulling ends the pass, runs the compute work, and begins it again:

Keep these true
  • The test must stay conservative. Compare the image with EngineConfig::occlusionCulling on and off: it must not change.
  • Reversed-Z: the Hi-Z keeps the min, and a draw is occluded when its nearest depth is smaller.
  • getHizLevelCount and getHizLevel must agree on the level sizes. HIZ_GROUP_SIZE, CULL_EARLY and CULL_LATE are defined in a shader and in gpuScene.cpp: change both.
  • No dispatch or barrier inside a render pass: RenderContext refuses them. That is why the pass is paused.
  • A call should keep its camera from frame to frame. Two cameras that swap call order read each other's flags: still correct, but slower.
Not done yet

No cluster or triangle culling, no single-pass Hi-Z build, no min/max sampler. The early culling runs before all other GPU work of the frame (D18 in docs/code_review.md). A draw that moves between the early and late pass can change which of two equal-depth surfaces wins (B24 showed this, with a camera that moved by float noise).
