Coordinate spaces and the view matrix

A vertex is stored in its mesh's own coordinates. Before it becomes a pixel, it passes through five more coordinate systems. Each step is one matrix or one simple formula. This page explains every space, which way its axes point, and how the camera's view matrix is built. The last part shows how RASM does it.

In short

  • The chain is object → world → view → clip → NDC → pixels. The model, view and projection matrices do the first three steps. The GPU does the last two.
  • A right-handed, Y-up world has X right, Y up and Z toward you. A camera there looks down its own −Z.
  • The model matrix is T · R · S: scale first, then rotate, then move.
  • The view matrix is the inverse of the camera's own world matrix. For a camera without scale it is RT · translate(−eye).
  • A quaternion stores a rotation in 4 numbers. Its inverse is its conjugate, and it blends smoothly (slerp).
  • Vulkan's clip space has Y down; OpenGL, Direct3D and Metal have Y up. Engines fix this with a negative viewport height or a flip in the projection.

1The chain of spaces

A space is a coordinate system: an origin and three axes. The same point has different numbers in different spaces. Rendering moves every vertex through a fixed chain of them. Each arrow below is one transform.

The same scene, as each space sees it:

Object (model, local)

Origin: the mesh's own pivot.

The numbers in the file. One mesh can be placed many times.

World

Origin: the scene's origin.

All objects, lights and cameras in one space. Lighting and physics often work here.

View (eye, camera)

Origin: the camera.

The world as the camera sees it. The camera sits at 0 and looks down −Z.

Clip

Origin: —

4D homogeneous coordinates. The GPU clips triangles against −w ≤ x, y ≤ w here.

NDC (normalized device coordinates)

Origin: the screen's center.

Clip divided by w. The visible part is a cube: x and y in −1..1, depth in 0..1 (or −1..1 in OpenGL).

Framebuffer (window, screen)

Origin: a corner of the image.

Pixel coordinates, and a depth value for the depth test.

2Axes and handedness

Three axes at right angles can be arranged in two ways that are mirror images of each other. Point X to the right and Y up. Then Z points either toward you or away from you. The first is right-handed, the second left-handed. Curl the fingers of that hand from X to Y, and the thumb shows Z.

Neither choice is better. What matters is that every part of a program agrees. Tools and file formats chose differently:

Tool or formatHandednessUp axis
glTF 2.0right+Y (a model's front faces +Z)
OpenGL tradition, Mayaright+Y
Blender, 3ds Maxright+Z
Unityleft+Y
Unreal Engineleft+Z

Converting between two of them is a fixed matrix:

A different up axis: a rotation

A rotation keeps the winding of every triangle.

A different handedness: a mirror

One negated axis. A mirror also reverses the winding of every triangle, so it changes face culling. See Face culling and winding order.

3Object to world: the model matrix

An object is placed in the world with three parts: a scale S, a rotation R and a translation T. With column vectors (the convention of OpenGL, glm and most math books), a point is transformed as p′ = M · p, and the model matrix is

M = T · R · S        p_world = T · (R · (S · p_object))

Read it from right to left: the matrix next to the point acts first. So the object is scaled around its own pivot, then turned, then moved. The order matters:

Swap the rotation and the translation, and the object lands somewhere else:

R · T: move, then rotate

The object turns around the world origin instead of its own pivot.

T · R: rotate, then move

The object turns around its own pivot, then moves. This is the order in M = T · R · S.

A hierarchy (a wheel on a car, a node tree in a glTF file) multiplies the matrices of all parents:

Row or column vectors

Some texts (classic Direct3D, DirectXMath) write p′ = p · M, with the point as a row. Then every matrix is the transpose, and products are read left to right: M = S · R · T. It is the same math with the other notation.

Row-major or column-major

A separate question: how the 16 numbers sit in memory. glm and GLSL store columns one after the other, so m[3] is the translation column. Mixing up the storage order transposes the matrix without any error message.

4Storing a rotation: quaternions

A rotation can be stored in several ways. Games most often use a unit quaternion: four numbers (w, x, y, z) with length 1. A rotation by the angle θ around the unit axis a is

q = ( cos(θ/2),  sin(θ/2) · a )        rotate a vector:  v' = q · v · q*

Why a quaternion: 4 numbers, and no special angles where it breaks.

Combine

Two rotations in a row are one product: q2 · q1 (q1 acts first).

Undo

The conjugate q* = (w, −x, −y, −z) turns back.

Blend

Slerp blends two rotations at an even speed.

Normalize

Rounding makes the length drift from 1 after many products: normalize it from time to time.

The other ways to store a rotation:

Euler angles (yaw, pitch, roll)

3 numbers. Good for user input and editors.

Problem: gimbal lock: at pitch ±90° two axes line up and one degree of freedom is lost. Blending is uneven.

3×3 matrix

9 numbers. Good for transforming many points; the GPU.

Problem: drifts away from a pure rotation; hard to blend

Axis and angle

4 numbers. Good for building a rotation.

Problem: combining two is awkward

Unit quaternion

4 numbers. Good for storing, combining and blending.

Problem: must be turned into a matrix for the GPU

5The view matrix

A camera is an object like any other. It has a position eye and a rotation R, so it has a world matrix C = T(eye) · R. That matrix takes points from the camera's space to the world. Rendering needs the opposite: points from the world into the camera's space. So the view matrix is the inverse:

V = C⁻¹ = (T · R)⁻¹ = R⁻¹ · T⁻¹ = Rᵀ · T(−eye)

Two facts make this cheap. The inverse of a translation is the translation by the negative vector. The inverse of a rotation matrix is its transpose (for a quaternion: its conjugate). No general 4×4 inverse is needed.

Building it with lookAt

Often the camera is given as "stand at eye, look at target, with up roughly up". The three axes of the camera follow from cross products:

lookAt breaks straight up and straight down

If f is parallel to up, f × up is zero: looking straight up or down breaks lookAt.

The rows of RT are the camera's axes, and the last column is RT · (−eye):

      |  r.x   r.y   r.z   −r·eye |
V  =  |  u.x   u.y   u.z   −u·eye |
      | −f.x  −f.y  −f.z    f·eye |
      |  0     0     0      1     |

Each row asks one question about a world point p: how far is it to the right (r), up (u), and behind (−f) the camera? A point in front of the camera gets a negative z.

6Clip space, NDC and pixels

The projection matrix turns view space into clip space. Its main job is to put the distance in front of the camera into w, so that the GPU can divide by it: far things get smaller. The details are in Projection and reversed-Z depth. Here we only need the last two steps, which the GPU does after the vertex shader:

NDC:     (x_n, y_n, z_n) = (x_c, y_c, z_c) / w_c

pixels:  x_p = vx + (x_n + 1) / 2 · width
         y_p = vy + (y_n + 1) / 2 · height        (the API's own y direction)
         depth = minDepth + z_n · (maxDepth − minDepth)

7Y up or Y down

The APIs do not agree on which way NDC y points:

OpenGL

NDC y = +1 is the top. NDC depth −1..1. Pixel row 0 is at the bottom.

Direct3D 11/12

NDC y = +1 is the top. NDC depth 0..1. Pixel row 0 is at the top.

Metal

NDC y = +1 is the top. NDC depth 0..1. Pixel row 0 is at the top.

Vulkan

NDC y = +1 is the bottom. NDC depth 0..1. Pixel row 0 is at the top.

Most math libraries (glm among them) build OpenGL-style matrices, where +Y is up in clip space. Feed such a matrix to Vulkan unchanged and the image comes out upside down. There are three common fixes:

No fix

Vulkan maps y = +1 to the bottom row. The image is upside down on screen, and every winding is reversed.

A: viewport flip

y = top + h, height = −h. Clip space stays Y-up, the same as on the other APIs. Shaders that compute clip positions by hand need no change.

Needs: Vulkan 1.1 (or VK_KHR_maintenance1)

B: projection flip

P[1][1] = −P[1][1]. Clip space becomes Y-down.

Cost: any full-screen pass or hand-written clip position must flip y too

Flip the final image

Render upside down and flip the final image.

Cost: a copy; rarely used

Fixes A and B both give an upright image, so a counter-clockwise triangle stays counter-clockwise on screen.

Mouse positions have the same problem the other way round: windows report y growing downward. Code that maps the mouse into NDC-like coordinates flips it: yn = 1 − 2 · ymouse / height.

9Pitfalls

The image is upside down, or every face is culled
A Y-up projection on Vulkan without a flip (section 7), or a flip done twice.
Objects turn around the world origin instead of their center
The matrix order is wrong: R · T instead of T · R.
The model is mirrored, or inside out
Data from a tool with the other handedness, converted with a rotation only.
A matrix "does nothing" or does something strange
Row-major data read as column-major: the matrix is transposed.
Lighting looks wrong on scaled objects
Normals transformed with the model matrix. They need the inverse transpose (Normal mapping).
Far from the origin, vertices jitter
A 32-bit float has only 24 bits of precision: at 100 km, steps are about 8 mm. Large worlds render relative to the camera.
The camera spins wildly at the top or bottom
lookAt with forward parallel to up, or Euler angles at gimbal lock. Clamp the pitch (Camera controllers).

10In RASM

RASM uses the glTF convention: a right-handed, Y-up world, and a camera that looks down its local −Z. Transforms are a position, a quaternion and a scale. Clip space is Y-up on every backend; the Vulkan backend flips Y with a negative viewport height (fix A).

Keep these true
  • Clip space is Y-up everywhere. Pass a normal, top-left rectangle to setViewport; the backend flips it. Do not flip again in a projection.
  • A shader that writes a clip position by hand uses Y-up (+1 is the top row).
  • Do not mirror imported data to change handedness. A mirror flips the winding, and face culling depends on it (Face culling).
  • glm config macros belong in CMake, not in a .cpp (B2).
  • Transform math is covered by tests/transformTests.cpp and tests/cameraTests.cpp.
Not done yet

All positions are 32-bit floats in world space. Large worlds need double precision on the CPU and camera-relative rendering (development plan, phase 5, map engines).
