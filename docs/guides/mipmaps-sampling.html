Mipmaps and texture sampling

When a pixel shader reads a texture, the GPU does not just fetch one texel. It works out how big the pixel is on the texture, picks a smaller copy of the texture that matches, and blends a few texels together. This page explains each step: why shrinking a texture flickers, how a mip chain fixes it, how mips are made, how the GPU chooses a level, and the filters and wrap modes that a sampler controls. The last part shows how RASM does it.

In short

  • When one pixel covers many texels, a single sample picks one of them almost at random. The result flickers and shows patterns: aliasing.
  • A mip chain stores the texture again at 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 … of its size, each level filtered from the one above. It costs only 1/3 more memory.
  • The GPU measures how fast the texture coordinates change between neighboring pixels (the derivatives) and picks the level λ = log2(texels per pixel).
  • Bilinear blends 4 texels inside one level; trilinear also blends two levels; anisotropic takes several samples along a stretched footprint.
  • Color mips must be averaged in linear light, not on the stored sRGB values.
  • The wrap mode decides what is read outside 0 to 1: repeat, mirror, or clamp.

1The problem: shrinking a texture

A texture is a grid of texels. The screen is a grid of pixels. When an object is close, one texel covers several pixels (magnification). When it is far away, one pixel covers many texels (minification). Minification is the hard case.

The right color for a pixel is the average of all texels under it. But a far-away pixel may cover hundreds of texels, and averaging hundreds of texels for every pixel is far too slow. The fix is to do the averaging once, ahead of time.

2The mip chain

A mipmap (from the Latin multum in parvo, "much in a small place") is a list of copies of the texture. Level 0 is the original. Each next level is half as wide and half as high, and each of its texels is the average of 2×2 texels of the level above. The chain stops at 1×1.

Sizes that are not a power of two work the same way: each level is the size above divided by 2, rounded down, but never below 1. A 1000 × 600 texture has 10 levels: 1000×600, 500×300, 250×150, 125×75, 62×37, 31×18, 15×9, 7×4, 3×2, 1×1. Rounding down means a level does not cover exactly 2×2 texels at the edges, which a good filter takes into account.

3Making the mips

The filter

The simplest filter is the box filter: each new texel is the plain average of the 2×2 texels above it. It is fast and good enough for many textures. Wider filters look at more texels and give sharper, cleaner results:

FilterTexels per output (1D)Result
Box2Slightly blurry; can keep some aliasing
Tent (triangle)4Smoother
Mitchell–Netravali, Kaiser, Lanczos4 to 8Sharper, less aliasing; Lanczos can add faint halos

Each level can be made from the level above (fast; small errors add up) or from level 0 directly (better; more work for the small levels). Near the edges a wide filter needs texels from outside the image. For a texture that tiles (a brick wall that repeats), it should take them from the opposite edge; otherwise the small levels show a seam.

Color must be averaged in linear light

Color textures are stored in sRGB: the stored number is not proportional to the light (see color spaces). Averaging the stored numbers gives the wrong brightness. The right way is: decode each texel to linear, average, encode the result.

4How the GPU picks a level

The GPU must know how many texels one pixel covers. It finds out from the neighbors. Pixel shaders always run in blocks of 2×2 pixels (a quad). Every pixel of the quad computes its texture coordinate uv, and the GPU subtracts them: the difference between horizontal neighbors is ∂uv/∂x, between vertical neighbors ∂uv/∂y. These are the derivatives.

The full rule, as the Vulkan and Direct3D specifications give it, for a texture of w × h texels:

ρx = sqrt( (∂u/∂x · w)² + (∂v/∂x · h)² )     texels per pixel, along screen x
ρy = sqrt( (∂u/∂y · w)² + (∂v/∂y · h)² )     texels per pixel, along screen y

λ  = log2( max(ρx, ρy) ) + bias
λ  = clamp(λ, minLod, maxLod)

λ ≤ 0   magnification: use level 0 with the magnification filter
λ > 0   minification: use level λ with the minification filter

Each step of λ by 1 is one level, because each level halves the size. λ is rarely a whole number; what happens with the fraction is the mip filter (section 5). The bias shifts every choice: a negative bias makes textures sharper (and noisier), a positive one blurrier.

Derivatives can break

The neighbors must run the same code. Inside an if that differs between the 4 pixels of a quad, some neighbors do not compute uv, and the derivative is undefined. A jump in uv also breaks it: at the seam of a texture atlas, or where uv wraps from 0.99 to 0.01, the GPU sees a huge change and picks the smallest level, which shows as a thin line of wrong color.

When there are no neighbors

Compute shaders and vertex shaders have no quads, so they have no automatic derivatives. There the shader names the level itself: SampleLevel(uv, lod), or passes its own derivatives: SampleGrad(uv, ddx, ddy). A plain Load(x, y, level) reads one texel with no filter at all.

5Filtering: nearest, bilinear, trilinear

The sample point almost never lands on the center of a texel. The filter decides what to return.

The mip filter adds a second choice: what to do with the fraction of λ. Nearest takes the closer level. Linear samples the two levels around λ and blends them by the fraction. Bilinear inside each level plus linear between levels is called trilinear filtering.

6Anisotropic filtering

On a floor seen at a low angle, a pixel's footprint is not a square: it is long and thin. It covers few texels across, but many texels along the view direction. λ uses the longer side, so the whole sample comes from a small, blurry level, even though the short side wanted a sharp one.

The usual rule (from the anisotropic filtering extension that most GPUs follow):

ρmax = max(ρx, ρy)      ρmin = min(ρx, ρy)
N    = min( ceil(ρmax / ρmin), maxAnisotropy )     number of samples
λ    = log2( ρmax / N )                            level of each sample

Each of the N samples is itself trilinear, so 16× anisotropy can mean up to 128 texel reads. GPUs are good at this, and they take fewer samples where the footprint is nearly square. The sampler sets maxAnisotropy; 8 or 16 is common.

7Wrap modes

Texture coordinates outside 0 to 1 are allowed. The wrap mode (or address mode) of the sampler decides which texel they read. It also matters inside the range: bilinear filtering at the edge reads one texel beyond it.

8Worked example

A floor uses a 1024 × 1024 texture, trilinear filtering and up to 8× anisotropy. Three pixels at three distances; click through them.

Pixel A: close to the camera

Up close, the only question is how to blend between texels. Bilinear gives smooth gradients; nearest gives visible squares.

Pixel B: in the middle

The pixel is a little bigger than a level 1 texel, so the result is almost all level 1. As the floor moves away, the weight slides smoothly toward level 2: no line where the level changes.

Pixel C: far away, at a grazing angle

Anisotropy keeps the floor sharp across the view, three levels finer than plain trilinear. This is why distant roads and floors look much better with it switched on.

10In RASM

RASM makes the full mip chain on the CPU, when the texture's pixels arrive, and uploads all levels in one host copy. Sampling uses six fixed samplers: trilinear with 8× anisotropy, or nearest, each with three wrap modes.

Keep these true
  • fillTexture must fill every level that createTexture allocated: the view covers all of them, and an unfilled level is read as garbage at a distance.
  • Color textures (_SRGB) take the sRGB resize path; data textures (_UNORM) take the linear one. The format decides, so a texture must be created with the right one (see color spaces).
  • Keep maxLod at VK_LOD_CLAMP_NONE. A sampler with the Vulkan default of 0 would use level 0 only.
  • Sample with automatic derivatives only outside branches that differ within a quad.
