Camera controllers and easing

A camera controller turns mouse moves, the wheel and held keys into a camera position and rotation, once per frame. This page explains the two common kinds, the fly camera and the orbit camera, and how to smooth their motion so it looks the same at any frame rate. The last part shows how RASM does it.

In short

  • A controller keeps its own state (angles, a center, a distance) and writes a target pose. Smoothing then moves the rendered pose toward it.
  • A fly camera stores yaw and pitch. Yaw turns around the world's up axis, pitch around the camera's right axis. Pitch is clamped short of ±90°.
  • An orbit camera turns around a center point. A turntable uses yaw and pitch; an arcball maps the mouse onto a sphere.
  • Mouse moves are distances: never multiply them by the frame time. Held keys are speeds: always multiply them by it.
  • Exponential easing with t = 1 − e−k·dt gives the same motion at 30 or 144 frames per second. A fixed factor per frame does not.

1What a controller does

Each frame, the controller reads the input of that frame and updates a pose: a position and a rotation. The view matrix is then built from the pose (see the view matrix). Good controllers split this into two poses:

2Reading the input

Mouse positions in units that do not depend on the window

A mouse move arrives in pixels. A move of 100 pixels is a big part of a small window and a small part of a big one. So controllers first map pixels to a resolution-independent range, often −1..1 like NDC. Windows report y growing down, so y is flipped at the same time.

s   = min(width, height)
x_n = (2 · x_px − width) / s
y_n = (height − 2 · y_px) / s          (flipped: up is positive)

Mouse moves are distances, keys are speeds

This is the most common mistake in camera code. A mouse move of 0.2 units is a distance the hand moved. At a high frame rate, the same hand motion is split into more, smaller moves; the sum is the same. So the turn angle is Δ · sensitivity, with no frame time in it.

A held key is different. It says "move while I am down", which is a speed. One frame at 144 fps is a quarter as long as one frame at 36 fps, so it must move a quarter as far: distance = speed · dt.

Mouse movedistanceangle = Δ · sensitivity× dt: looking gets slower as the frame rate rises
Wheeldistance (notches)zoom by notches · step× dt: the same
Held keyspeedmove = speed · dtno dt: moving gets faster as the frame rate rises

Frame time, and held keys

  • Frame time dt is the time since the previous frame, from a monotonic clock. A debugger stop or a window drag can make one frame last seconds, so engines cap dt (for example to 0.1 s). Otherwise the camera jumps far on the next frame.
  • Held keys are read as a state ("is W down now?"), not as events. Read the physical key (a scancode), not the letter: WASD then sits in the same place on an AZERTY keyboard.
  • When the window loses focus, it misses the key-up event. Reset the key state then, or the camera keeps flying.

3The fly camera

A fly camera (also called FPS or free camera) turns where the mouse goes and moves with the keys. Its state is two angles, yaw and pitch, plus a position. There is no roll: the horizon stays level.

Why pitch is clamped

At pitch ±90° the view direction is parallel to the up axis. Then yaw and roll turn around the same line, and one degree of freedom is lost: this is gimbal lock. In practice the view flips over or spins when the mouse crosses the pole, and a lookAt built from this direction gets a zero cross product (lookAt). Clamping to a value a little below 90° avoids all of it.

Moving with the keys

The forward and right directions come from the rotation: forward = R · (0, 0, −1), right = R · (1, 0, 0). Each held key adds or subtracts one of them. Two keys at once (W and D) give a diagonal of length √2 ≈ 1.41, so the sum is normalized first; otherwise diagonal moves are 41% faster.

move = 0
if W: move += forward      if S: move −= forward
if D: move += right        if A: move −= right
if |move| > 0:  position += normalize(move) · speed · dt

Fly or walk

With forward taken from the full rotation, looking down and pressing W moves down: a fly camera. A walking camera uses the yaw alone for movement, so it stays on the ground, and adds separate keys for up and down.

Drag or capture

Games capture the mouse: it is hidden, and every move turns the view. Tools and viewers usually turn only while a button is held, so the mouse stays free for the rest of the user interface.

4The orbit camera

An orbit camera looks at a center point (a model, a map location) from a distance. Dragging turns the camera around the center; the wheel changes the distance. There are two classic ways to map the drag to a rotation.

How an arcball maps the mouse to a sphere

Imagine a glass ball of radius 1 under the screen, centered in the window. Each mouse point is lifted straight up onto the ball. Two points p0 (where the drag started this frame) and p1 (where it is now) give a rotation: around the axis p0 × p1, by the angle between them.

DetailChoices
The angleShoemake's arcball turns by twice the angle between the points (it uses the quaternion p1 · p0*). Many simple trackballs use the angle itself, times a sensitivity.
Turn the object or the camera"Grab" feel: the scene follows the cursor. For a camera, that means turning the camera the opposite way: by −angle, in its own frame.

ZoomAdditive (distance −= notches · step) feels slow far away and too fast up close. Multiplicative (distance ×= 0.9 per notch) feels the same at any distance. Clamp at a minimum either way.

5Smoothing: exponential easing

Raw input is jumpy: a mouse reports whole pixels, and frames are not evenly spaced. Smoothing moves the current pose a fraction t of the remaining way to the target each frame:

current = current + (target − current) · t

The question is how to choose t. A fixed number (say 0.1) looks right at one frame rate and wrong at all others: at 144 fps it runs 144 times a second, at 30 fps only 30 times. The fix comes from asking what the smoothing should do in time, not in frames: the remaining distance should shrink by the same factor every second.

remaining(time) = remaining(0) · e^(−k · time)

one frame of length dt:   remaining ← remaining · e^(−k · dt)
so                         t = 1 − e^(−k · dt)

Many frames multiply: e−k·dt1 · e−k·dt2 · … = e−k·(dt1 + dt2 + …). Only the total time matters, not how it was cut into frames. k is the speed: half the distance is gone after ln 2 / k seconds (0.12 s for k = 6).

Rotations and orbits

Positions blend with a straight mix. Rotations should not: mixing two quaternions number by number and normalizing moves at an uneven speed. Slerp (spherical linear interpolation) moves along the arc at a constant speed, and takes the same t.

Stop when close enough

Exponential easing never reaches the target exactly. Worse, mixing two nearly equal values still rounds a little, by an amount that depends on dt, so a camera "at rest" moves by float noise. Snap to the target once the difference is below a tiny threshold.

A smoother option: a spring

Exponential easing starts at full speed and then slows down. A critically damped spring also keeps a velocity, so the camera speeds up gently too. It costs a few more lines and one more state variable per value.

8In RASM

RASM has both kinds of controller: FREE_FLY (a fly camera, turned while a mouse button is held) and TRACK_BALL (an arcball-style orbit camera). The engine reads the input itself, once per frame, inside beginFrame. Easing is the exponential kind of section 5.
