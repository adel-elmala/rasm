Drawing

Vector lines, polygons and points

A map is mostly vector data: roads and rivers are lines, lakes and parks are polygons, shops are points. Their shape comes from the data, but their look comes from a style: a road is 7 pixels wide at any zoom. This page shows how to draw them on the GPU: lines spread from their center line in the vertex shader, joins and caps, smooth edges from the distance to them, polygons with holes, points, the order they are drawn in, how they lie on terrain, and what culling must do for widths measured in pixels. The last part shows how RASM does it.

In short

  • A line's vertices keep its center line, and an offset in half widths. The vertex shader multiplies the offset by the style's width, so a style changes without a rebuild.
  • Where two segments meet, a join fills the gap (miter, bevel or round); the ends get a cap (butt, square or round).
  • Each fragment knows its distance to the edge. One pixel's change of it (fwidth) gives a smooth edge, at any width and angle.
  • A line is never drawn under one pixel: a thinner one keeps one pixel and fades by its coverage, so it neither vanishes nor shimmers.
  • A width is in pixels or in metres, spread across the screen or in the ground's plane.
  • Polygons with holes are cut into triangles by ear clipping. Points are quads with a round edge.
  • Layers are drawn in a set order, over the ground by a small depth bias, or over everything.
  • On terrain, features are cut on the terrain's grid, so each piece lies flat on one terrain triangle.
  • Culling must pad a feature's bounds by its width in pixels at its distance, or wide lines vanish at the screen's edge.

1The problem

A GPU draws triangles. A road is a list of points, and its width is a number of pixels on screen: as the camera zooms out, the road keeps its 7 pixels while the world shrinks. So the triangles of a line depend on the camera. Rebuilding them on the CPU every frame is slow; building them once in world units makes them too thin far away and too wide near.

Triangles in world units

The width follows the perspective: a far road is thinner than a pixel and breaks up.

A center line and offsets

The data is built once; the vertex shader spreads it by the width every frame.

2A line as a center line and offsets

Each segment of the line becomes a quad of four vertices. All four sit on the center line, two at each end. Each also has an offset (x along the segment, y across it), in half widths: −1 on one side, +1 on the other. The vertex shader puts the vertex at anchor + (x · along + y · across) · half width.

3Joins and caps

Two quads that meet at an angle leave a wedge open on the outer side of the turn. A join fills it. The ends of a line get a cap.

Miter

The outer edges meet in a point. A sharp turn makes a long spike, so past a limit it becomes a bevel.

Bevel

The two outer corners joined by one triangle.

Round

A fan of triangles around the joint; its edge is round to the pixel.

4Smooth edges, and never thinner than a pixel

Each vertex hands its offset to the fragments, interpolated. A fragment's distance to the edge is |y| on the body, the length of (x, y) in a round join or cap, the larger of |x| and |y| in a square cap. The geometry reaches one pixel past the edge, and the fragment covers what is inside:

coverage = saturate((1 − distance) / fwidth(distance) + 0.5)

distance
in half widths: 1 at the edge
fwidth
how much the distance changes over one pixel: the smooth band is one pixel wide at any width, distance and angle

A line of 0.3 pixels, drawn as it is

Only the pixels whose centers it crosses are hit: dashes that crawl and shimmer.

One pixel, faded to 0.3

Every pixel along it gets a little: a faint, steady line. MSAA and TAA then have nothing to fight.

5Pixels or metres; the screen or the ground

Pixels

A road of a map style: the same width at any zoom. The world width is the pixel width times the pixel's size at that depth.

Metres

A river 40 m wide, as it is in the world. Far away it reaches the one-pixel minimum and fades.

Across the screen

The offset is turned to the screen: the line never foreshortens. For 3D paths, routes, markers.

In the ground's plane

The band lies on the ground: a road draped on hills. Seen edge-on it gets thinner, down to the minimum.

6Polygons with holes

A lake is an outline with islands inside, each a ring of points. Ear clipping cuts it into triangles: it joins each hole to the outline by a bridge, so the shape is one ring, then cuts off "ears", triangles made of three neighbour points with no other point inside, until nothing is left. A polygon's own edges are triangle edges, so MSAA or TAA smooths them.

7Points and markers

A point is a quad around its anchor, its four corners at offsets (±1, ±1). The fragment's distance is the length of its offset, so the disc is round and smooth; a ring along its edge is the same distance test at 1 − ring width. An icon is the same quad with texture coordinates from an atlas.

8Draw order and depth

Map layers overlap on purpose: a road over a park over the ground. All lie in the same plane, so the depth test cannot order them. They are blended with their smooth edges, in a fixed order of layers, each one a little nearer than the ground by a depth bias. An overlay (a route, a selection) is drawn at the near plane, over everything.

A marker at its anchor's depth

A quad that faces the screen stands up from the ground: below its anchor the ground is nearer and hides it.

The ground's depth under each corner

Each corner takes the depth of the ground under its pixel, through the ground plane at the anchor, when that is nearer.

9Draping on terrain

A road on hilly terrain must follow the drawn ground exactly, or it sinks into a hill or floats over a valley. The terrain is drawn as two triangles per cell, each flat. So cut every segment where it crosses a grid line or a cell's diagonal, and lift each new point by the terrain's own rule: then each piece lies flat on one terrain triangle. Polygons are cut the same way.

10Culling a width in pixels

A line's bounding sphere is built from its center line. A line 40 pixels wide whose center line is just off the screen still covers 20 pixels of it. A pixel is larger far away, so the pad grows with depth: pad the sphere by the widest offset in pixels times the size of a pixel at the sphere's far side, for each view. The occlusion test then uses the padded sphere too.

11Motion for TAA

TAA moves each pixel's history by the motion of what is in it. A smooth edge is part line, part ground: the line writes its anchor's motion scaled by its coverage, and the blend keeps the rest of the motion behind it. So a line over the sky, the ground or a moving object reprojects without a smear.

12Pitfalls

Thin lines break into crawling dashes
They are thinner than a pixel. Keep one pixel and fade by the coverage.
Wide lines vanish at the screen's edge
Culling uses the center line's bounds. Pad by the width in pixels at the far side, per view.
Markers and roads are cut in half on a tilted map
A quad facing the screen sinks into the ground below its anchor. Give each corner the depth of the ground under it.
A see-through line is darker at its joints
Its quads and joins overlap on the inner side of a turn and blend twice. Draw see-through lines opaque, or as one layer with a lower alpha.
Roads sink into hills or float over valleys
The line is not cut on the terrain's grid, or the terrain is drawn from other heights than the ones used to drape.
Layers flicker against each other
Same depth, no order. Draw layers in a fixed order, blended, with a depth bias against the ground only.
Mesh simplification ruins the lines
LODs and meshlets are for meshes whose vertices stay where they are. Build vector meshes without them.

13In RASM

A layer of vector features is an ordinary entity: a mesh built by the vector builders (include/rasm/vector.h), drawn with a MaterialType::VECTOR material whose VectorMaterial is the style. So it goes through the GPU culling, the Transparent pass's sort, TAA and MSAA like any blended mesh, and is a TileStreamer tile's content like any other. Labels are vector features too: a fourth kind of vertex, the glyph, with its own page, Text and labels.

any thread the renderer's thread shaders 
// From examples/25_vector_map/main.cpp: a road layer, then a tile's roads built on a server thread.
rasm::MaterialHandle roads = makeLayer(engine, {.color = glm::vec4(1.0f, 0.75f, 0.25f, 1.0f), .width = 7.0f, .outlineColor = glm::vec4(0.55f, 0.32f, 0.08f, 1.0f), .outlineWidth = 1.5f, .drawOrder = -10});
// ... on a server thread:
rasm::VectorGeometry geometry;
rasm::addVectorLines(geometry, origin, points.data(), sizes.data(), static_cast<uint32_t>(sizes.size()), {.join = rasm::LineJoin::ROUND, .cap = rasm::LineCap::ROUND});
data.layers[ROADS] = rasm::buildVectorMesh(std::move(geometry));
// ... on the main thread, when the tile arrives:
const rasm::MeshHandle mesh = engine.createMesh(std::move(data.layers[ROADS]));
const rasm::EntityHandle entity = engine.createEntity("layer", mesh, roads, rasm::Transform{origin});

Keep these true
  • src/scene/vector.cpp and shaders/lib/vector.slang agree on the vertex: the kinds, the metrics, the across direction cross(along, up).
  • No offset reaches past MAX_VECTOR_MITER half widths: culling's pad counts on it.
  • Vector meshes have no LODs and no meshlets (buildVectorMesh).
  • Every pass that draws a VECTOR material spreads its vertices, the pick pass too (Picking): it compiles vertMain with RASM_PICK and writes the feature id. The feature id's bits are in tangent.w: copy them, never compute with them.
  • A vertex behind the camera takes the sizes of one at the near plane (getSafeW): a line that crosses the near plane keeps a bounded width.
