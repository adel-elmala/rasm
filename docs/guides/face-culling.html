Face culling and winding order

In a closed mesh, about half the triangles face away from the camera and are hidden by the other half. Face culling throws them away before they are rasterized. The GPU decides which side a triangle shows from the order of its corners on screen. This page explains that test, the conventions around it, and the cases that reverse it. The last part shows how RASM does it.

In short

  • A triangle's winding is the order of its three corners on screen: counter-clockwise or clockwise. Seen from behind, the same triangle has the opposite winding.
  • The GPU finds the winding from the sign of the triangle's signed area in screen space, after projection.
  • The application says which winding is the front (glTF and OpenGL use counter-clockwise), and which faces to cull: none, back or front.
  • A mirroring transform (negative determinant) reverses the winding of every triangle. So does showing the image upside down.
  • Double-sided surfaces (leaves, cloth, glass panes) are not culled, and their back side is lit with the normal reversed.

1Why cull back faces

Every triangle of a closed mesh (a box, a character, a car body) has an outside. Its normal points out. From any camera position, the triangles whose normals point away from the camera are behind the ones that point toward it. Drawing them only costs time: the depth test hides them anyway.

2Winding: the order of the corners

A triangle is stored as three indices into the vertex list, in a fixed order: 0, 1, 2. Look at the triangle and follow the corners in that order. You go either counter-clockwise or clockwise. Walk around to its back and look again: the same order now goes the other way.

3The test: the signed area

After the vertex shader, the divide by w and the viewport transform, each corner has a 2D screen position (xi, yi). The shoelace formula gives the area of the triangle with a sign:

a = ½ · [ (x0·y1 − x1·y0) + (x1·y2 − x2·y1) + (x2·y0 − x0·y2) ]

    = ½ · ((x1 − x0)·(y2 − y0) − (x2 − x0)·(y1 − y0))

The screen-space test gives the same answer as the 3D question "does the normal point toward the eye?", that is (p0 − eye) · n < 0 for a front face, where n is the cross product of two edges. Projection keeps the side of the eye a plane is seen from. The 3D form is what a program uses before the GPU, for example to cull whole groups of triangles.

5What reverses the winding

A mirroring transform

A mirror (a reflection) turns a counter-clockwise triangle into a clockwise one. A scale of −1 along one axis is a mirror, and so is any matrix whose 3×3 part has a negative determinant. Two mirrors cancel: a scale of (−1, −1, 1) is a 180° rotation.

Mirrors are common: a level designer reuses a left door as a right door with a negative scale, and glTF files may contain nodes with negative scale. Because the determinant is a product, a hierarchy is mirrored when an odd number of its matrices are.

An upside-down image

Showing an image upside down is also a mirror (across the horizontal axis). This is what happens when Y-up clip coordinates reach Vulkan, whose clip space is Y-down, without a fix: every winding reverses. Both usual fixes, a negative viewport height or a flipped projection, give an upright image and keep the windings as authored (Coordinate spaces).

6Double-sided surfaces

A leaf, a flag or a sheet of paper is one layer of triangles. It must be visible from both sides, so its material turns culling off. The back side then needs care in lighting: its normal still points out of the front.

7Where culling sits in the pipeline

8Worked example

The triangle of section 3, drawn with Vulkan into an 800 × 600 image with back-face culling and a counter-clockwise front face. Vulkan computes the area in framebuffer coordinates (rows go down) with a minus sign in front: a = −½ · Σ (xi · yi+1 − xi+1 · yi). A positive a is counter-clockwise.

1. In Y-up clip space

This is how the projection matrix produced it (clip space with Y up, as in OpenGL-style math).

2. With a negative viewport height

The flip of the viewport and the minus sign of Vulkan's formula cancel: Y-up counter-clockwise stays counter-clockwise.

3. Without the flip

This is why a Y-up projection on Vulkan needs a fix, or a clockwise front face.

4. A mirrored object

The determinant decides the cull mode per object, before drawing. Nothing about the triangle itself changes.

10In RASM

RASM uses glTF's convention: counter-clockwise is front. The cull mode is chosen per instance on the CPU from the material and the determinant, and the draw list is grouped so that each group of equal modes is one indirect draw. Because the Vulkan backend flips Y with a negative viewport height, clip space and the screen agree on the winding (D8).

Keep these true
  • The counter-clockwise front face and the negative-height viewport are a pair. Change the Y flip, and the winding rule changes with it.
  • getCullMode and the shader's handedness must agree: both come from the sign of the world matrix's determinant.
  • Every pipeline takes its cull mode from the command buffer. A pass that draws must set it (beginPass sets NONE); code that changes it puts NONE back.
  • Anything that changes a transform or a material's doubleSided must go through updateCullModes, or a draw stays in the wrong run.
  • The draw list has one run per cull mode: adding a mode means changing MAX_DRAW_RUNS on both the C++ and the Slang side (Struct layout).
