Normal mapping and tangent space

Normal mapping makes a flat surface look bumpy. It does not move any vertex. It only changes the normal that lighting uses, pixel by pixel, with values read from a texture. This page explains how the normals are stored, the small coordinate frame they are stored in, and the sign and transform details that are easy to get wrong. The last part shows how RASM does it.

In short

  • Lighting depends on the normal. A normal map stores a normal per texel, so a flat triangle can be lit like a detailed surface.
  • Each channel stores one component: value = (n + 1) / 2. The texture must be read as plain numbers (UNORM), never as sRGB.
  • The normals are in tangent space: x along the texture's u, y along its v, z out of the surface. The TBN matrix turns them into world space: n = T·x + B·y + N·z.
  • Meshes store T and a sign w = ±1. The bitangent is B = w · cross(N, T). The sign handles mirrored UVs.
  • Normals move to world space with the inverse-transpose of the model matrix; tangents move with the model matrix itself. A mirroring transform flips the sign.
  • Without stored tangents, a shader can build the frame from screen-space derivatives of the position and the UV.

1Why normal mapping

The brightness of a lit surface depends on the angle between its normal and the light (n·l, see PBR shading). Small details like bricks, scratches or pores change that angle many times inside one pixel-sized area. Modelling them as real triangles would need millions of triangles. Normal mapping keeps the triangles few and stores only the normals of the detail, in a texture.

A normal map is usually baked: an artist builds a detailed mesh with millions of triangles, and a tool records its normals into a texture laid over a simple mesh. It can also be painted, or made from a height map.

2Storing a normal in a texture

A unit normal has 3 components, each between −1 and 1. A texture channel stores 0 to 1 (as 0 to 255 in 8 bits). So each component is moved and scaled into that range, and back when it is read:

stored = (n + 1) / 2          n = stored · 2 − 1

3Tangent space

In which coordinate system are the stored normals? There are two choices.

Object space

The normal is stored in the mesh's own coordinates. Simple to use: rotate with the object and done. But the map works only for that one mesh, it cannot tile or be reused, and it breaks when the mesh bends (skinning). It uses all colors, so it compresses badly.

Tangent space (the usual choice)

The normal is stored relative to the surface itself: z is "straight out of the surface". The same brick map then works on a wall, a floor or a curved column, it tiles, and it bends with the mesh. Since z is always positive, two channels are enough (section 9).

5Where tangents come from, and the sign w

For one triangle, the tangent and bitangent follow from its positions and UVs. Take the two edges e1 = p1 − p0, e2 = p2 − p0, and the UV differences (Δu1, Δv1), (Δu2, Δv2). We look for the directions T (where u grows) and B (where v grows) with

e1 = Δu1 · T + Δv1 · B
e2 = Δu2 · T + Δv2 · B

r = 1 / (Δu1 · Δv2 − Δu2 · Δv1)
T = r · (Δv2 · e1 − Δv1 · e2)
B = r · (Δu1 · e2 − Δu2 · e1)

Here B is where v grows. That is the texture's "up" when v grows toward the top of the image (the OpenGL habit). When v grows downward, as in glTF, where (0, 0) is the top-left corner, the texture's up is −B. Each vertex then averages the tangents of the triangles around it (Lengyel's method). Storing all three of T, B, N per vertex costs 9 floats. Most formats store N, T and one more number instead: the tangent sign w, either +1 or −1. The shader rebuilds the bitangent:

B = w · cross(N, T)

cross(N, T) is at right angles to both, so it already points along B or exactly against it. w says which one. Why can it be −1? Because of mirrored UVs.

MikkTSpace: one answer for everyone

A normal map is only correct with the exact tangents that the baking tool used. Different averaging rules (by area, by angle, split or not at UV seams) give slightly different tangents, and the result shows as seams and wrong shading on curved areas. MikkTSpace (Morten Mikkelsen, 2008) fixes this by being one precisely defined algorithm that bakers and engines all implement the same way. Blender, Unity, Unreal and Substance use it, and the glTF format says that a mesh without tangents should get MikkTSpace tangents.

6Interpolation, and Gram–Schmidt

The vertex shader passes N and T to the fragment shader, and the rasterizer interpolates them across the triangle. Interpolated unit vectors are a little too short, and T is no longer exactly at right angles to N. The fix is the Gram–Schmidt step: remove the part of T that points along N, then normalize.

7Moving normals and tangents to world space

Vertices move to world space with the model matrix M. Tangents lie in the surface, like a small edge, so they move the same way: Tworld = M · T (the 3×3 part, no translation). Normals do not. If M scales one axis more than the others, M · N is no longer at right angles to the surface:

Why it works, in one line: N ⊥ T means NTT = 0. After the transform we need N′T (MT) = 0. With N′ = (M−1)T N, the product is NT M−1 M T = NTT = 0.

Mirroring transforms

A model matrix with a negative determinant mirrors the object (for example a scale of −1 on one axis, used to place a left door from a right door). The mirror keeps T and N correct, but it turns cross(N, T) around: the frame changes handedness, just as with mirrored UVs. So the sign must change too: wworld = w · sign(det M).

8A frame without stored tangents

Some meshes have no tangents. Instead of computing them on load, the fragment shader can build a frame on the fly from screen-space derivatives. Upside

No tangents to compute, store or transform. Mirrored UVs and mirroring transforms work by themselves, because the frame comes from what is on screen.

Downside

The frame is flat per triangle, so it does not match a baked map on curved, smooth surfaces as well as MikkTSpace does. The derivatives are coarse at triangle edges and noisy at grazing angles. If the UVs do not change (det = 0), there is no frame at all.

9Two-channel maps and strength

A tangent-space normal points out of the surface, so z > 0. And it has length 1. So z can be computed from x and y:

z = sqrt(max(0, 1 − x² − y²))

Many formats also have a strength (glTF calls it scale): a number that multiplies x and y before the vector is normalized. Values above 1 make the bumps stronger; 0 makes the surface flat.

12In RASM

RASM does tangent-space normal mapping in the fragment shader, in getNormalFromMap (shaders/lib/pbr.slang), which shadePBR calls when the material has a normal texture. It supports both kinds of frame from this page: the stored tangent with its sign (sections 5 to 7), and the frame from derivatives (section 8) when the mesh has no tangents.
