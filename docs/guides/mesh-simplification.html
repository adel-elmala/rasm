Mesh simplification

A far object covers a few pixels, but its mesh still has all the triangles it has up close. Mesh simplification makes coarser copies of a mesh, its levels of detail (LODs), that look almost the same from far away. This page explains the classic algorithm: edge collapse driven by quadric error metrics. The last part shows how RASM does it. Choosing a level each frame is a separate topic: see LOD selection.

In short

  • An edge collapse merges the two ends of an edge into one vertex. The two triangles on that edge disappear.
  • A quadric is a 4×4 matrix per vertex. It gives the sum of squared distances from any point to the planes of the triangles around that vertex.
  • The algorithm collapses the cheapest edge first, adds the quadrics of the two ends, and repeats until a target count or an error limit.
  • The cost is a distance: how far the surface moved, in the mesh's own units. That is what makes it usable for choosing a level later.
  • Borders and UV seams need extra care, or holes grow and textures tear.
  • If every collapse keeps one of the two old vertices, all levels can share one vertex buffer. Then a level costs only its indices.

1Why fewer triangles

Drawing a far object with all its triangles wastes work in three places:

CostWhy tiny triangles hurt
Vertex workEvery vertex is fetched and shaded, even when 50 vertices land in one pixel.
Pixel workGPUs shade pixels in 2 × 2 blocks (quads). A triangle that covers one pixel still runs the pixel shader up to 4 times. Many tiny triangles can cost several times the pixels they cover.
AliasingDetail smaller than a pixel flickers as the camera moves. A coarser mesh can even look better.

The LODs are built once, offline or when the mesh loads. Each frame, the renderer only picks one per object (see LOD selection).

Edge collapse with quadric error metrics, published by Michael Garland and Paul Heckbert in 1997, is the standard. The rest of this page explains it.

3Edge collapse

Where does the merged vertex go? There are two choices:

Full collapse

Both ends move to a new point, chosen to fit the old surface best (section 5 shows how to compute it). Best quality. But the result has new vertices, so each level needs its own vertex data.

Half-edge collapse

One end moves onto the other end, which stays where it is. A little lower quality. But every vertex of every level is one of the original vertices, so all levels can share one vertex buffer (section 10).

Collapses that must be refused

Some collapses break the mesh. Before each one, the algorithm checks:

CheckWhat goes wrong without it
Flip: does any triangle around the moved vertex turn over (its normal points the other way)?A fold in the surface: a dark crease or a hole in the silhouette.
Link condition: do u and v share neighbours other than the two triangles on their edge?Two surfaces get glued at one point; the mesh is no longer a clean surface (non-manifold).
Border and seam rules (section 7)Holes grow, cracks open, textures tear.

4The cost: how far would the surface move?

We need a number for each possible collapse: how much it changes the shape. Measuring the true distance between the old and the new surface is slow. The quadric trick estimates it with a few multiplications.

Start from one triangle. Its plane is 4 numbers p = (a, b, c, d), with a unit normal (a, b, c). Write a point as v = (x, y, z, 1). The distance from the point to the plane is p · v. Its square can be written as a matrix product:

distance²(v) = (p · v)²
             = vᵀ (p pᵀ) v
             = vᵀ K v          K = p pᵀ, a symmetric 4×4 matrix

Now the trick. Each vertex gets the sum of the K of the triangles around it:

Q(vertex) = K1 + K2 + … + Kn      one K per triangle that uses the vertex
cost(v)   = vᵀ Q v                sum of squared distances to them

Q is still one 4×4 symmetric matrix: 10 numbers, no matter how many planes it sums. At the vertex's own position the cost is 0, because the vertex lies on all its planes. Moving it away costs more in some directions than others:

The cost of one collapse

To collapse the edge (u, v), add the two quadrics and evaluate them at the place where the merged vertex will be:

Q      = Q(u) + Q(v)
cost   = targetᵀ Q target
target = u for a half-edge collapse, the best new point for a full one

After the collapse, the merged vertex keeps Q = Q(u) + Q(v). So a vertex's quadric always holds the planes of all the original triangles it replaced. Later costs are measured against the original surface, not against the last simplified one. That is why errors do not quietly add up step after step.

5The algorithm

  1. Quadrics. For every triangle, compute its plane and K. Add K to the quadric of each of its 3 vertices. Many implementations weight K by the triangle's area, so big triangles count more.
  2. Costs. For every edge, compute the cost of collapsing it. Put the edges in a priority queue (a min-heap), cheapest first.
  3. Collapse. Take the cheapest edge. If a check from section 3 refuses it, skip it. Otherwise collapse it and add the two quadrics.
  4. Update. The edges around the merged vertex changed: compute their costs again and fix their place in the queue.
  5. Stop when the triangle count reaches the target, or the next cost is above an error limit, or the queue is empty.

The same idea works in 2D, on a line instead of a surface. There, each point's "planes" are the lines of its two segments. Click through a simplification of 7 points down to 4. The numbers are the cost of each point's cheapest collapse, as a distance (the drawing is 6 units wide).

1. Every point gets a quadric and a cost

P1 sits almost on the straight line from P0 to P2, so it can go for 0.10. P3 and P4 are the top of the hill: removing either changes the shape a lot.

2. Collapse the cheapest: P1 onto P2

P2 now holds the quadric of P1 too, so it "remembers" the original line through P1. Its cost went up: moving it now would also move the part of the line that P1 used to hold.

3. Next: P5 onto P4

Only the neighbours of the merged point get new costs. Everything else stays in the queue as it was.

4. P3 onto P4, then stop

The level's error is the cost of its largest collapse, 0.55. Because the quadrics remember the original line, this number already compares the result with the original, not with the last step.

The best new position

For a full collapse, the best target is where vT Q v is smallest. Split Q into its top-left 3×3 part A, the first three numbers of its last column b, and its bottom-right number c. The cost is xTAx + 2 bTx + c, and its minimum is where A x = −b. That is one 3×3 linear system. When A cannot be inverted, for example on a flat area where every point of the plane costs 0, fall back to the cheapest of the two ends or the midpoint.

Absolute or relativeIn the mesh's units (meters, if the mesh is in meters), or divided by the mesh's size (0.01 = 1% of its size)Choosing a level on screen needs absolute units. Relative is handy as a quality setting: "at most 1% deformation".

What the error coversposition only, or also normals and UVsPosition only can hide a big change in shading. See the next section.

A quadric gives the sum of squared distances to many planes, so its square root is an estimate, not an exact distance. In practice it is close to the largest deviation, and it is the number simplifiers usually report.

7Borders, seams and attributes

Open borders

A border edge has a triangle on one side only: the edge of a sheet, or the rim of a hole. Here the quadric fails. On a flat sheet, every point of the plane costs 0, so a border vertex can slide into the sheet for free. The border is eaten away.

Locking matters when one object is split into several meshes that meet along a border, for example a model split into parts by material. If each part is simplified and chosen on its own, an unlocked border can move differently in each part, and a crack opens between them. Locked borders always match, at any mix of levels. The cost: the border keeps all its vertices, so a part with a long border cannot get very coarse.

UV seams and attributes

A vertex is more than a position: it has a normal and UVs. Where a texture is cut open (a UV seam), or a hard edge has two normals, the same position is stored as two vertices with different attributes.

ApproachHowResult
Position-only quadrics, seam rulesFind vertices with equal positions. Treat their edges like borders: they only collapse along the seam, together.No tears. But a collapse inside one island can still bend its normals or stretch its texture.
Attribute quadricsExtend the quadric with the attributes (Garland and Heckbert 1998, Hoppe 1999): the cost also counts how far the normal and UV would change.Better shading and texture. Bigger quadrics, slower, and the weights between position and attributes need tuning.

8Targets and when to stop

A simplifier gets a target: an index count (3 per triangle), an error limit, or both. It stops at whichever comes first. It can also stop early, above the target: when every remaining collapse is refused (locked vertices, seams, the link condition), there is nothing left to do.

RuleTypical choiceWhy
Each level's targethalf the triangles of the level beforeAn object twice as far covers a quarter of the pixels; halving per level gives many levels for little memory.
Minimum sizeno levels for small meshes; stop below a few dozen trianglesA level of a 200-triangle mesh saves almost nothing.
Stallingstop when a step removes less than, say, 10%The simplifier is stuck on constraints; another level would be a near copy.
Maximum levelsa fixed number, e.g. 8Fits a fixed-size table on the GPU.

With halving, the extra indices form a geometric series: ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + … < 1. All the coarser levels together cost less than LOD 0's indices.

10Sharing vertices between levels, and the memory cost

With half-edge collapses, every vertex a level uses is an original vertex. So a level is only a new index list into the same vertex buffer.

Worked example: the memory cost

A mesh with 100,000 triangles and 50,000 vertices of 64 bytes, with 32-bit indices. Seven coarser levels, each half the one before:

PartSize
Vertices: 50,000 × 64 B3.20 MB
LOD 0 indices: 100,000 × 3 × 4 B1.20 MB
LOD 1 to 7 indices: 99,219 triangles × 12 B1.19 MB, +27% on the whole mesh
If each level had its own vertices too: about 49,600 more × 64 B+3.18 MB more, +99% in total

Sharing the vertex buffer makes the whole chain cost about a quarter of the mesh instead of doubling it. The price is the quality of half-edge collapses, and the scattered fetches of coarse levels.

After simplifying

Each level's triangles come out in the order the collapses left them. Reorder them for the GPU's vertex cache (so a vertex shared by neighbouring triangles is shaded once), like any other index buffer.

11In RASM

RASM builds LODs for every sub-mesh as it loads, with the meshoptimizer library: buildMeshLods calls meshopt_simplify, which is the quadric edge collapse from this page, with half-edge collapses onto existing vertices.

Keep these true
  • Every level indexes the sub-mesh's own vertices: they share one vertexOffset. Per-level vertices would need a new field in GeometryLod.
  • Errors never get smaller from one level to the next, and LOD 0's is 0. selectLod assumes that a coarser level never has a smaller error.
  • Borders stay locked, or sub-meshes that meet can crack.
  • MAX_MESH_LODS is the same in include/rasm/mesh.h and core.slang. A change to GeometryData follows the struct contract.
  • buildMeshLods runs on worker threads: it must not touch the engine.
