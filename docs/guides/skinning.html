Skinning: a mesh that bends with its skeleton

A character is one mesh, its skin, over a tree of joints, its skeleton. Node animation moves joints; skinning moves each vertex of the skin with the joints near it, so knees and elbows bend smoothly. This page explains the bind pose, the joint matrices, the weights and the blend, where in the frame to do the work, how to cull a mesh whose shape changes every frame, and its motion vectors. The last part shows how RASM skins once per frame on the GPU.

In short

  • The mesh is modelled once, in the bind pose. Each joint stores its inverse bind matrix: the inverse of its world matrix in that pose.
  • A joint's joint matrix is world(joint now) · inverseBind. It moves a bind-pose vertex the way the joint moved since the bind pose.
  • A vertex follows up to 4 joints, with weights that add up to 1: v' = Σ wᵢ · Jᵢ · v. This is linear blend skinning.
  • Skin once per frame in a compute pass, into a buffer of posed vertices that every view reads: the camera and each shadow map. Skinning in the vertex shader repeats the work in every view.
  • The bounds: a sphere per joint around the vertices it moves, moved by its joint matrix, and the sphere around all of them.
  • Motion vectors need the vertex's place in the frame before: keep last frame's posed vertices too.

1The bind pose and the joint matrix

An artist models the skin once, with the skeleton in one pose: the bind pose, often arms out in a T. The vertices are stored in that pose. To pose the skin, each vertex is taken into its joint's own space as it was in the bind pose, then out again with the joint's matrix of today:

The joints are nodes of the same tree that node animation moves (see the node tree). So the joint matrices cost one multiply per joint after the world matrices are known, and the skeleton can have any depth.

The mesh's own node does not place it

The joints' world matrices already put the skin in the scene. glTF says a skinned mesh ignores the matrix of the node that holds it; only the object's own placement (where the character stands) is applied on top.

2Weights: a vertex follows several joints

A vertex near the elbow should move partly with the upper arm and partly with the forearm. Each vertex stores up to 4 joints and 4 weights. Its posed place is the weighted mix of the places its joints put it:

v′ = w₀·J₀·v + w₁·J₁·v + w₂·J₂·v + w₃·J₃·v

w₀ + w₁ + w₂ + w₃ = 1

Jᵢ
the joint matrix of the vertex's joint i
wᵢ
its weight; weights that do not add up to 1 shrink or grow the vertex, so scale them to 1

The sum of matrices Σ wᵢ·Jᵢ can be built first and applied once, to the position and, as a normal matrix, to the normal. This is linear blend skinning. It is cheap and it is what glTF asks for. Its known faults:

One joint per vertex

Rigid parts: the skin tears open outside the joint and folds into itself inside.

Blended near the joint

The vertices in a band around the elbow mix both joints, so the skin curves round the bend.

Twist: the candy wrapper

Mixing two matrices turned far apart shortens the vertices: a twisted wrist or a bent elbow loses volume.

A vertex may name more than 4 joints (glTF allows more sets of 4). Keep the 4 largest weights and scale them to add up to 1 again: the lost weights are small, and 4 fit one vector.

3Where to skin: once per frame, for every view

A frame draws a skinned character many times: in the camera's view and in every shadow map that sees it. A sun with 4 cascades and the camera make 5 views; a point light's cube adds 6 more. There are two places for the skinning math:

In each view's vertex shader

Each draw reads the base vertex and the joint matrices and skins again. Fine for one view; the cost grows with every shadow map.

Once, in a compute pass

A pass before the views writes each posed vertex, with the same layout as the base vertex. The views read those instead.

The posed copy has a cost in memory. It holds the whole vertex (position, normal, tangent and UVs), and with two frames in flight there is one copy per frame slot, so the frame that draws never reads a buffer the next frame writes:

4Bounds of a mesh that changes shape

Culling needs a sphere that holds the mesh. A rigid mesh has one sphere, computed once, moved by its matrix. A skinned mesh changes shape every frame: an arm reaches out past the bind-pose sphere. Computing a new sphere from all the posed vertices would cost a pass over the vertices before culling. A cheaper bound that is still safe:

  1. At import Per joint: a sphere around the bind-pose vertices that joint moves (weight above 0).
  2. Each frame Per joint: move its sphere by its joint matrix. The center is J · c; the radius is r times the matrix's largest stretch.
  3. Then The sphere around all moved spheres: the center of their box, the radius to the farthest edge.

5Motion vectors of skinned vertices

TAA and motion blur need, per pixel, where the surface was in the frame before (see TAA). For a rigid mesh, the frame before's matrix is enough. A skinned vertex also changes its place inside the mesh, so the matrix does not tell where it was. The posed buffers already hold the answer: there is one per frame slot, and the other slot holds the frame before's posed vertices.

6Pitfalls

The character lies on its back, or stands far from its place
The mesh node's matrix was applied on top of the joints'. A skinned mesh ignores its own node's matrix.
The skin explodes into spikes
Joint indices read with the wrong type (glTF stores them as 8 or 16-bit integers), or matrices in the wrong order: world · inverseBind, not the other way round.
Parts of the skin grow or shrink as they move
The weights do not add up to 1. Scale them to 1 at import, after keeping the 4 largest.
Elbows and wrists get thin when they bend or twist
Linear blend skinning loses volume. Add helper joints, or use dual quaternion skinning.
An arm disappears at the edge of the screen
The mesh was culled with its bind-pose sphere. Use bounds that follow the joints.
Shadow maps cost much more with a character in them
Each shadow view skins the mesh again in its vertex shader. Skin once per frame and let every view read the result.
A walking character smears under TAA
Its motion vectors use this frame's posed vertex for both frames. Keep the frame before's posed vertices.

7In RASM

RASM skins on the GPU, once per frame. The import puts each skinned vertex's joints and weights into a stream of the geometry pool, beside the 64-byte vertex. The Animate pass (node animation) also writes the joint matrices and each posed sub-mesh's bounds. The Skin pass then writes the posed vertices, which the culling and draws of every view read. The same pass first adds a mesh's morph targets, so a morphed mesh with no skin goes through it too.

Nothing new in the API: an entity with a skinned mesh plays a clip like any animated entity.

const rasm::MeshHandle fox = engine.loadGLTF("models/gltf/Fox/Fox.glb");
engine.waitForLoads();
const int32_t run = engine.findAnimation(fox, "Run");
if (run >= 0)
    engine.playAnimation(entity, {.clip = static_cast<uint32_t>(run)});

Keep these true
  • Every shader that reads a draw's vertices goes through getVertex, never SceneData::vertsPtr directly, or it draws a skinned mesh in its bind pose.
  • A skinned vertex has the base vertex's layout, so every view draws it with the same code.
  • Both slots' skinned vertex buffers grow together, after the GPU is done with them: a frame reads the other slot's.
  • The weights of a SkinVertex add up to 65535 exactly.
  • The skin stream is welded and reordered with its vertices, like the color stream.
  • Morph before skin: the deltas are in the bind pose.
