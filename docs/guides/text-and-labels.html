Text and labels

A map names its places: towns, streets, mountains. The names stand at points in the world, but they are read on the screen: they keep their size in pixels, face the viewer, stay sharp at any size, and never cover each other. This page shows how: glyphs stored as distances to their edge, a halo from the same distances, text laid out from the font's metrics, labels spread across the screen from a world anchor, a placement each frame that keeps the most important names, and what depth, MSAA and TAA need. The last part shows how RASM does it.

In short

  • A font's glyphs go into an atlas of signed distances to their edge, not of coverage. Distances blend into straight lines, so a magnified edge stays one pixel wide.
  • A fragment turns the distance into screen pixels and covers what is inside: coverage = saturate(distance + 0.5). A halo is the same test a few pixels further out.
  • Text is laid out with a pen: each glyph moves it by its advance, and kerning moves pairs such as "AV" closer.
  • A label has a world anchor; its glyph quads are spread around the anchor's point on the screen, in pixels.
  • Each frame, each view places its labels: the highest priority first, each one only where no placed box is. Equal labels keep the winner of the last frame, and labels fade in and out.
  • A label is shown or hidden whole, by whether its anchor is in front of what the scene drew there. It moves with its anchor and has no TAA jitter, so it stays sharp.

1The problem

Text on a map has three needs that ordinary textures do not meet. (The stats overlay's text is another case: fixed-size debug text from bits, see Text from a bitmap font.)

Sharp at any size

One atlas serves 10 px and 60 px text. A scaled bitmap blurs; distances do not.

In the world, on the screen

The name stands at a world point but faces the viewer, upright, at a size in pixels.

Never on top of each other

A dense map has more names than room. Each frame keeps the important ones that fit.

2Glyphs as distances

An atlas texel can store how much of it the glyph covers (a bitmap), or its signed distance to the glyph's edge: positive inside, negative outside. The texture filter blends neighbour texels in a straight line. A blend of coverage is a soft ramp; a blend of distances is still a distance, so the edge, where the distance is 0, stays a sharp line between the texels.

The fragment shader turns the stored value back into a distance in screen pixels, then covers the part of the pixel that is inside. How many screen pixels one atlas texel spans comes from how fast the atlas coordinate moves across the screen (fwidth), so it works for any size and any tilt:

screenRange = range · (screen pixels per atlas texel)

distance = (value − 0.5) · screenRange

coverage = saturate(distance + 0.5)

value
the bilinear sample of the atlas, 0 to 1
screen pixels per atlas texel
1 / fwidth(texel coordinate): 2 when the text is drawn twice as large as the atlas
coverage
the fragment's alpha: 1 more than half a pixel inside, 0 more than half a pixel outside, a one-pixel ramp across the edge

A distance atlas is small: 32 texels per em is enough for map text from 8 to 60 pixels. Very large text shows the atlas's own limits: the corners of a glyph become slightly round, because one distance per texel cannot hold a sharp corner. A multi-channel distance field (MSDF) stores three distances per texel and keeps the corners; it reads the same way, through the median of the three.

3The halo

A label over a busy map needs a halo: a band of a second color around each glyph, so the text reads over roads and water. It is the same test, further out: saturate(distance + halo + 0.5). It can reach only as far as the atlas stores distances: half the range, in screen pixels.

Halo with each glyph

Close glyphs overlap: a halo drawn after a neighbour's fill covers part of it.

All halos, then all fills

Each label emits its glyph quads twice: the halo's first, in the outline color, then the fill's.

4Laying out a line of text

Text arrives as UTF-8 bytes. They are decoded into codepoints (one to four bytes each), and each codepoint picks its glyph in the atlas. A glyph knows its quad (where it sits from the pen, and its size) and its advance, how far it moves the pen. A kerning pair moves two glyphs closer or apart.

Where the text sits around its anchor is a choice of the style:

Center

A region's name over its middle.

Bottom

The text's bottom over the point: a summit's name.

Left

The text's left beside the point: a shop's name by its marker.

5In the world, on the screen

Every vertex of a label holds the same world point, its anchor, and its glyph corner in ems. The vertex shader projects the anchor, then moves the vertex across the screen by the corner times the text's size in pixels. The text faces the screen and keeps its size whatever the distance. A size in metres instead divides by the size of a pixel at the anchor, so the text shrinks with distance like the ground does.

6Placing labels

Each frame and each view, the labels' boxes are projected to the screen and placed greedily: sort them, then walk the list and place each one whose box touches nothing placed before it. A grid of small cells makes the test cheap: a placed box marks its cells, and a later box is free when none of its cells is marked.

  1. Projecteach label's anchor, and its box in pixels from the text's size
  2. Sorthigher priority first; among equals, the ones placed last frame first
  3. Testthe box's cells: all free, or one taken by a placed box
  4. Mark and fadea placed box marks its cells; each label fades toward shown or hidden

Stable

Equal labels keep last frame's winner, then a fixed order. A moving camera does not make them swap every frame.

Fade

A label fades in and out over a fraction of a second, so a change of placement never pops.

Per view

Each view has its own screen, so each places its labels itself, and remembers its own fades.

7Shown or hidden whole

A label must hide behind a hill or a nearer building. The first idea is the depth test: give the text the depth of its anchor and let each pixel be tested. That cuts labels apart. The text is flat on the screen, but the world behind it is not: a hill beside the anchor rises in front of part of the text, a post or a box edge crosses it, and the label is torn, a word missing its first letters. When the camera moves, the cut moves with it and the text flickers.

So a label is tested once, at its anchor, and drawn whole or not at all. After the scene's opaque draws, and before the labels, a small pass reads the depth they left around each label's anchor. The anchor is moved toward the camera by a few pixels' size first, so the ground it stands on does not hide it, and it is tested at the 3 x 3 pixels around its own: shown when it is in front of one of them, so a thin wire or the edge of a roof right at the anchor does not make it blink. The answer fades the label in or out over a moment, as placement does, and the text is then drawn over everything.

Placement on the CPU still runs before the depth is known: a label hidden behind a building keeps its room on the screen, so it can still push a visible label out.

8Sharp under MSAA and TAA

The glyph's edge is smoothed by its own distance, so MSAA adds nothing and takes nothing away. TAA is the risk: it moves the camera by a sub-pixel jitter each frame and blends the frames, which softens edges that already are smooth. So text is drawn without the jitter, on the same pixels every frame, and the blend of identical frames is the frame itself. When the camera moves, each glyph writes the motion of its anchor, so the history follows the text instead of smearing it.

9Culling text measured in pixels

A label's draw is culled by the bounds of its anchors. Its text reaches past them, in pixels, so the cull test pads the bounds by the farthest the text may reach (a number of ems) times the text's size, at the far side of the bounds. A label whose anchor is just off screen keeps its draw while its text may still show.

10Pitfalls

Large text is blurry
The atlas holds coverage. Store distances and turn them into coverage over one screen pixel.
The halo is cut off in a box around each letter
The halo reaches past the distances the atlas stores. Use a larger range, or a thinner halo for small text.
A letter's right side is covered by its neighbour's halo
Halo and fill are drawn glyph by glyph. Draw all of a label's halo quads first, then its fills.
Labels flicker when the camera moves
Equal labels swap places frame to frame. Prefer last frame's winner among equals, fade, and sort with a fixed tie-break.
Text shimmers or looks soft with TAA
It takes the jitter and the history blends it. Draw it without the jitter, and give it its anchor's motion.
Labels are torn by hills, posts and edges
Each pixel of the text is tested against the depth behind it. Test the anchor once, and draw the label whole or not at all.
A label on a slope blinks out
Its anchor lies on the ground, at the ground's own depth. Move it toward the camera by a few pixels' size before the test, and test a few pixels around it.
Names in Arabic or Devanagari look wrong
Those scripts need shaping: glyphs that change by their neighbours, joined, reordered. A codepoint-to-glyph layout cannot do it.

11In RASM

Labels are vector features (Vector lines, polygons and points): a mesh built by addVectorLabels and drawn with a MaterialType::VECTOR material whose VectorMaterial::font names a font. Its width is the text's size, its outlineColor and outlineWidth the halo. So labels go through the GPU culling, the blend sort, MSAA and TAA like the other vectors, and are a TileStreamer tile's content like any mesh. What is new is the font atlas and the placement each view runs on the CPU.

Exports An export places its labels once, on the whole image's pixels, with the text, the halo, the depth bias and the collision cells times its pixel scale (placeLabels takes the cell size), and with no fade. A survey frame per tile tests each anchor in the one tile that keeps it, at once; the tiles then keep those fades, so a label that crosses a tile border is drawn whole in both.
any thread the renderer's thread, each frame shaders 
// From examples/25_vector_map/main.cpp: the font once, a label layer, then a tile's names on a server thread.
rasm::FontAtlas fontAtlas;
if (!engine.loadFont("fonts/Roboto-Medium.ttf", fontAtlas))
    return 1;
const rasm::FontHandle font = engine.createFont(fontAtlas);
layers[PLACE_NAMES] =
    makeLayer(engine, {.color = glm::vec4(0.15f, 0.12f, 0.1f, 1.0f), .width = 13.0f, .outlineColor = glm::vec4(1.0f, 1.0f, 1.0f, 0.9f), .outlineWidth = 2.0f, .drawOrder = 40, .font = font});
// ... on a server thread:
names.push_back({.position = place, .text = features.placeNames[p].c_str(), .priority = 1.0f, .anchor = rasm::LabelAnchor::LEFT, .offset = glm::vec2(0.2f, 0.0f)});
rasm::addVectorLabels(geometry[PLACE_NAMES], origin, *features.font, names.data(), static_cast<uint32_t>(names.size()));

Keep these true
  • No glyph corner reaches past MAX_LABEL_REACH ems from its anchor, or culling may drop its draw at the screen's edge. It must equal VECTOR_LABEL_REACH.
  • A label's halo quads come before its fill quads in the index buffer.
  • A mesh with labels has no meshlets (buildVectorMesh): its instance's meshletVisibility holds its first label slot.
  • stb_truetype trusts a font's tables: load only fonts you trust.
