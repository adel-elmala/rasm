PBR shading

Physically based rendering (PBR) gives each pixel the color that real light would give it. It splits what a surface does to light into a few small formulas, each based on physics: how much light bounces off the top, how much goes in and comes out again, and how a rough surface spreads it. This page explains each formula and why it is there. The last part shows how RASM does it.

In short

  • The color of a pixel is the light that leaves the surface toward the eye. The rendering equation adds up the light that arrives from every direction, weighted by the BRDF and by the angle.
  • The BRDF has two parts. Diffuse: light that goes into the surface and comes out in all directions (Lambert: color / π). Specular: light that bounces off the top.
  • Specular uses microfacets: the surface is many tiny mirrors. Cook–Torrance = D · G · F / (4 (n·l)(n·v)).
  • D (GGX): how many mirrors face the right way. G (Smith): how many are not hidden. F (Schlick Fresnel): how much each mirror reflects, more at grazing angles.
  • The metallic-roughness workflow needs 3 inputs. Non-metals reflect about 4% (F0 = 0.04) and have a colored diffuse. Metals reflect their base color and have no diffuse.
  • A surface never sends out more light than it gets: that is energy conservation.

1The question a shader answers

When a pixel is shaded, the shader knows one point p on a surface. It must answer one question: how much light leaves p toward the eye, and in which color? Four directions describe the situation. All of them are unit vectors that start at p.

Older shading models (Phong, Blinn–Phong) answer the question with formulas that look good but are made up. They have numbers like a "shininess exponent" that do not exist in the real world, and a material that looks right under one light often looks wrong under another. PBR uses formulas that follow how light behaves. The benefit is practical: a material made once looks right in every lighting, and artists set values they can measure, like "this is gold" or "this is 60% rough".

2The rendering equation, in plain words

James Kajiya wrote down the full answer in 1986. It is called the rendering equation:

Lo(p, v) = Le(p, v) + ∫ f(l, v) · Li(p, l) · (n·l) dl

∫ = over the hemisphere above p

Making it small enough for a shader

The integral has no closed form in general. Real-time shaders make it simple with one assumption: light comes from a few punctual lights, ideal points or directions with no size. Light from such a light arrives from exactly one direction, so the integral turns into a sum, one term per light:

Lo = Le + Σ f(li, v) · Ei · (n·li)      (Σ over the lights i)

Ei = the light's color × intensity × attenuation

The light from everything else (the sky, light bounced off walls) is added as a separate ambient term, see section 11. The rest of this page is about f, the BRDF.

3The BRDF and energy conservation

BRDF means bidirectional reflectance distribution function. It takes two directions, in (l) and out (v), and returns how much of the light from l leaves toward v. When light hits a surface, two things can happen:

So the BRDF is a sum of two parts:

f(l, v) = kd · f_diffuse  +  f_specular

The weight kd keeps the sum honest. Light that the top layer reflected as specular is not there any more to go in and come out as diffuse. This rule is called energy conservation: a surface that is not a light source can never send out more light than it receives. Old models broke it often: a shiny Phong highlight could add light on top of a full diffuse. In PBR the specular part takes its share first, and diffuse gets what is left.

Common choice for kdMeaning
1 − metallicmetals have no diffuse; the specular part is not taken out
(1 − F) · (1 − metallic)also remove the light that Fresnel reflected (section 8). Closer to energy conservation; a little darker at grazing angles

4Diffuse: Lambert

Light that goes into a non-metal bounces around many times before it comes out. By then it has "forgotten" where it came from, so it leaves equally in all directions. A surface that does this is called Lambertian, and its BRDF is a constant:

f_diffuse = c / π

c = the diffuse color (albedo), from 0 to 1 per channel

Why divide by π? Imagine light that arrives with the same strength from every direction of the hemisphere. A white surface (c = 1) should send all of it back out, no more and no less. Adding up the cosine term over the whole hemisphere gives ∫ cos θ dω = π. So the BRDF must be 1/π to send out exactly 1. Without the π, a white surface would send out π ≈ 3.14 times what it received.

5Specular: microfacets

A mirror reflects light in exactly one direction. Most real surfaces are not mirrors: their highlight is a soft blob, not a sharp point. Microfacet theory explains why. Under a microscope, the surface is made of many tiny flat mirrors, the microfacets, each facing a slightly different way. Each one is a perfect mirror. Together they spread the light.

The facets that matter are the ones whose normal is the half vector h = normalize(l + v). Robert Cook and Kenneth Torrance (1982) turned this idea into the specular BRDF that almost every engine uses today:

                 D(h) · G(l, v) · F(v, h)
f_specular  =  ───────────────────────────

Many engines merge G and the denominator into one visibility term V = G / (4 (n·l)(n·v)). Then the specular BRDF is just D · V · F, and the division by small numbers at grazing angles disappears into a formula that is safe to compute.

6D: the normal distribution (GGX)

The normal distribution function D says how the facet normals are spread around the surface normal n. The standard choice is GGX, also called Trowbridge–Reitz (Walter and others, 2007; Trowbridge and Reitz, 1975):

                     α²
D(h)  =  ─────────────────────────────
          π · ((n·h)² · (α² − 1) + 1)²

α is the roughness of the facets. A small α means almost all facets face n: D is a tall, thin spike, and the highlight is small and bright. A large α spreads them: D is low and wide, and the highlight is big and dim. At n·h = 1, the formula gives its peak, 1 / (π α²).

Roughness: what artists set, and what the formula uses

Artists set a perceptual roughness r from 0 (mirror) to 1 (fully rough). If α = r directly, most of the visible change happens near 0, and the slider feels uneven. Squaring it fixes that: α = r² (Burley, 2012). The glTF format defines its roughness this way too.

At r = 0 the peak is infinite. A real shader must avoid that: it clamps r to a small minimum, for example 0.045, so that the numbers stay inside a 32-bit float and the highlight of a tiny point light does not flicker from pixel to pixel.

8F: Fresnel

Look at a lake. Straight down, you see the bottom: water reflects only about 2% of the light. Look across it at a low angle, and it is a mirror. Every material does this. The share of light that a surface reflects grows toward 100% as the angle becomes grazing. This is the Fresnel effect, after Augustin-Jean Fresnel.

The exact Fresnel equations need the material's index of refraction and work with polarized light. Christophe Schlick (1994) found a cheap approximation that is close enough:

F(θ) = F0 + (1 − F0) · (1 − cos θ)⁵

cos θ = v·h  (the same as l·h)

F0 is the reflectance when you look straight at the facet (θ = 0). It is the one number that describes the material's specular color. The angle is measured to the facet normal h, not to n: that is the facet that does the reflecting.

F is a color, not a single number: F0 has three channels. For a non-metal they are equal (the highlight is white). For a metal they differ, and the highlight takes the metal's color. At 90° every material reflects everything, so the color fades to white at the edges.

A material needs a diffuse color, an F0 and a roughness. Real materials fall into two groups that use these very differently:

Non-metals almost all sit near 0.04, and metals have no diffuse at all. So one color and one switch are enough. The metallic-roughness workflow (used by glTF, Unreal and many others) stores three values per pixel:

Upside

Few inputs, hard to get wrong. A non-metal cannot get an F0 that does not exist in nature. Textures pack well: glTF puts roughness in the green channel and metallic in the blue channel of one texture.

Downside

All non-metals share F0 = 0.04. Water, gems or car paint need a different value. The specular-glossiness workflow stores F0 as its own color instead; glTF extensions such as KHR_materials_specular and KHR_materials_ior add it back.

Attenuation: the inverse-square law

A point light sends its light out over a sphere. At distance d the sphere has area 4πd², so the light per area falls as 1 / d². Twice as far means a quarter of the light. That is physics, and PBR uses it as it is.

The problem: 1 / d² never reaches 0. Every light then touches every pixel in the scene, and a renderer that sorts lights into tiles or clusters cannot skip any of them. Engines add a range: a distance where the light ends. To end it smoothly, they multiply by a window that is 1 near the light and falls to 0 at the range (Karis, 2013):

attenuation(d) = 1 / d²  ·  clamp(1 − (d / range)⁴, 0, 1)²

Physical light units are a separate choice. A physical engine gives a point light in lumens or candela and the sun in lux, and divides by 4π where needed. Many engines just use a unitless "intensity" that the artist tunes by eye.

11Ambient light, image-based lighting, emission

Punctual lights are only part of the light in a scene. The sky and every wall that bounces light also light the surface, from all directions at once. Several ways to add that light exist, from cheap to expensive:

MethodWhat it addsCost
Constant ambienta small fixed light × diffuse color. Keeps shadows from being pure black.nothing
Hemisphere ambienta sky color from above, a ground color from below, mixed by the normalalmost nothing
Image-based lighting (IBL)light from an environment image. Diffuse: a blurred "irradiance" map or spherical harmonics. Specular: a map pre-blurred per roughness, plus a small lookup texture for the BRDF (the split-sum method, Karis 2013).a few texture reads
Global illuminationreal bounced light: light maps, probes, or ray tracinglarge

Ambient occlusion (AO)

A texture or pass that says how much of the sky each point can see. Cracks and corners see less. AO darkens the ambient light only. Direct light from a punctual light is not affected: that is the job of shadows.

Emission

The Le term. The emitted color is added to the result as it is, with no BRDF and no lights. A screen or a lamp shade glows even in a dark room. Emission does not light other objects unless something else (a light, GI, bloom) makes it.

The result is HDR

The sum can be far above 1: a highlight can be 50 times brighter than a wall. A screen shows 0 to 1. So the result goes through tone mapping, a curve that squeezes bright values into the range a screen can show. The simplest is Reinhard: c / (c + 1), which maps 1 to 0.5 and 3 to 0.75. Others (ACES, AgX) keep colors and contrast better. Then the linear value is encoded for the display, see Color spaces.

12Worked example

One pixel, by hand. A grey plastic surface: base color 0.8, metallic 0, roughness r = 0.5. One directional light with E = 1. The light and the eye are both 30° from the normal, on opposite sides. So h = n: the eye sees the center of the highlight.

Now move the light to 50° while the eye stays at 30° on the other side. Then h is 10° away from n: D falls from 5.09 to 2.41 and the specular from 0.067 to 0.043. The diffuse hardly changes, but n·l drops to 0.643, and the pixel becomes 0.185. Make the same surface gold (F0 = 0.9, metallic 1) in the first setup: the diffuse is 0, the specular is 5.093 · 0.330 · 0.900 = 1.51, and the pixel is 1.31. That is above 1, which is normal for a highlight on metal; tone mapping takes care of it.

14In RASM

RASM shades every non-unlit material with one function, shadePBR in shaders/lib/pbr.slang. The uber shader's fragMain (in shaders/passes/rasm.slang) fills a PBRInfo with the fragment's position, normal, tangent, UVs, the scene's lights and the material row, and calls it. The BRDF is the textbook one from sections 4 to 8, in the form Filament uses.

Keep these true
  • Light, Material and SceneData are CPU↔GPU structs: a change to them follows the struct layout rules, on both sides.
  • Color and emissive textures are R8G8B8A8_SRGB; metallic-roughness, normal and occlusion textures are R8G8B8A8_UNORM. The shader expects linear values from all of them.
  • shadePBR outputs linear color. The sRGB attachment encodes it; do not apply the sRGB curve in the shader.
  • A light's direction points from the light toward the surface.
Not done yet

From the development plan, phase 4: an HDR color target with a separate tone-map pass (and Reinhard removed from the material shader), shadows, spot lights (B15), image-based lighting, alpha modes (the pipeline has no blending, so base color alpha does nothing today), and clustered lighting to remove the fixed limit of 8 lights.
