Shadow mapping

A point is in shadow when something stands between it and the light. Shadow mapping answers that question with two passes: first draw the scene's depth as the light sees it, then, for each pixel the camera sees, compare its depth from the light with that stored depth. This page explains the idea, the math, the artifacts and their fixes. The last part shows how RASM does it.

In short

  • Pass 1 draws the scene from the light into a depth-only texture, the shadow map. Each texel keeps the depth of the surface nearest the light.
  • Pass 2 draws from the camera. For each pixel, it moves the surface point into the light's view and compares: farther than the stored depth means something is in front of it, so it is in shadow.
  • A directional light (the sun) uses an orthographic light camera; a spot light a perspective one; a point light six of them (a cube).
  • The map has a finite number of texels, so a surface can shadow itself: shadow acne. A small depth bias and a normal offset fix it.
  • One comparison per pixel gives hard, jagged edges. PCF averages several comparisons for a soft edge.

1The question: can the light see this point?

Lighting a surface point needs the direction to the light and the surface normal. That alone lights every point that faces the light, even one behind a wall. A shadow is a visibility question: is the path from the point to the light free?

Ray tracing answers it directly, one ray per pixel and light. Rasterization has no rays, but it has a fast way to answer "what is the nearest surface along each line of sight": the depth buffer. Shadow mapping uses that from the light's point of view.

So shadow mapping is two passes:

3The light's camera

The shadow pass needs a view and a projection, like any camera. They follow from the kind of light.

LightIts raysLight cameraMaps
Directional (sun)parallel, from far awayOrthographic, looking along the light direction. Its box must hold every object that can cast a shadow into the view.1 (or several cascades, section 7)
Spotfrom a point, inside a conePerspective at the light, field of view = the cone's angle1
Pointfrom a point, all directionsSix perspective cameras, 90° each, one per cube face6 (a cube map)

For the orthographic light camera, the box is a design choice. Too large, and each texel covers a lot of ground: blurry, blocky shadows. Too small, and objects outside it cast no shadow. The near plane must sit behind every caster, or casters between the light and the near plane are clipped away and their shadows vanish.

4The test, step by step

The light camera's projection times its view is one matrix, S. In pass 2, for a fragment at world position p:

c     = S · (p, 1)                    light clip space
ndc   = c.xyz / c.w                   divide (w = 1 for orthographic)
uv    = (ndc.x · 0.5 + 0.5,  0.5 − ndc.y · 0.5)     texture coordinates, v = 0 at the top
d     = ndc.z                         the fragment's depth as the light sees it
stored = shadowMap(uv)                the nearest depth along that texel's ray

lit   = d is not behind stored        (standard depth: d ≤ stored; reversed-Z: d ≥ stored)
  • The uv line assumes a Y-up clip space and textures with row 0 at the top. An API or engine with other conventions flips v differently; the shadow pass and the lookup just have to agree.
  • With reversed-Z (near = 1, far = 0, see Projection and reversed-Z depth), a nearer surface has the larger depth, so the comparison flips.
  • A point outside the map (uv outside 0..1, or d outside the depth range) has no information. It is usually treated as lit.

5Shadow acne, and the fixes

The map stores one depth per texel: the depth at the texel's center. A surface tilted toward the light changes depth inside one texel. Half of the surface under each texel is then "behind" the stored value, and shadows itself in stripes. That is shadow acne.

The usual fixes, often combined:

FixHowCost of too much
Constant depth biasCompare against the stored depth moved by a small amount toward the fragment.Peter-panning: the shadow detaches from the object's foot, which seems to float.
Slope-scaled biasA bias that grows with the surface's slope as the light sees it. GPUs can add it while drawing the shadow map (the rasterizer's depth bias).Same, on steep surfaces only.
Normal offsetLook up the map from p + n · k, a point pushed off the surface along its normal, with k about one texel in world units, more where the light grazes the surface.Shadows shift slightly sideways; thin objects can leak light.
Draw back faces onlyThe shadow pass culls front faces, so the stored depth is the far side of closed objects.Fails for open meshes and thin walls.

Both offsets are best measured in texels, not in fixed world units: then they stay right when the map's size or the light camera's box changes.

6Soft edges: percentage-closer filtering

One comparison gives 0 or 1, so each shadow-map texel becomes a hard square on screen, and the shadow's edge is a staircase. Blurring the depths does not help: the average of two depths is a depth no surface has. Percentage-closer filtering (PCF) blurs the results instead: compare against several texels around the point, and average the 0s and 1s.

A wider kernel gives a softer edge at more reads per pixel. Real penumbrae grow with the distance between the caster and the receiver; techniques like PCSS (percentage-closer soft shadows) estimate that distance first and widen the kernel to match.

7Resolution, coverage and cascades

A shadow-map texel covers box width / map size world units. Near the camera, one texel can cover many screen pixels: blocky shadows (perspective aliasing). Far away, many texels fall into one pixel and are wasted.

VariantIdeaWhen
One map, fixed boxThe box covers a known area (a level, a room, a small scene).Small, bounded scenes; the simplest.
Box fitted to the viewEvery frame, fit the box around the part of the camera's frustum that can receive shadows. Snap it to whole texels so edges do not crawl as the camera moves.Medium scenes.
Cascaded shadow maps (CSM)Split the camera's frustum by distance into 3 or 4 slices, each with its own map: small, sharp boxes near the camera, large ones far away. The shader picks the cascade by the fragment's depth.Large outdoor scenes; the standard for a sun.
Virtual shadow mapsOne huge sparse map, only the pages that pixels need are drawn.High-end engines.

9Pitfalls

SymptomUsual cause
Stripes or moiré on lit surfacesShadow acne: no bias, or a bias smaller than half a texel's depth change.
Objects float; shadows start away from their feetToo much bias (peter-panning).
A shadow is missing near the lightThe caster is in front of the light camera's near plane, or outside its box.
Everything is in shadow, or nothing isThe comparison has the wrong direction (reversed-Z flips it), or v is flipped between the passes.
Shadow edges crawl when the camera movesA box fitted to the view but not snapped to whole texels.
Blocky shadows close to the cameraOne map stretched over too large an area: shrink the box, raise the resolution, or use cascades.
Reading the map gives garbage or validation errorsThe depth texture was not moved from attachment to sampled between the passes, or was not created sampleable.

10In RASM

RASM has one shadow map per shaded view, for one directional light the application names, from an orthographic light camera it places. Both passes are ordinary render graph passes from addScenePasses, so the graph orders them and adds every barrier. examples/06_shadows shows it, followed by a post pass, with no barrier in the example.

Keep these true
  • sampleShadow and shadePBR both use SceneData::shadowLight; neither picks a light by itself.
  • The offsets assume an orthographic light camera, so addScenePasses takes no other. A perspective one (spot lights) needs the texel size from the fragment's distance instead.
  • The light camera should look along the named light: the map's shadows fall along the camera, the bias follows the light.
  • The test direction follows reversed-Z. Changing the depth convention changes it too (Projection and reversed-Z depth).
  • The scene passes must declare readDepth on the shadow map, or they get no barrier and read it while it is still an attachment.
  • shadowTexture is the bindless index of this frame slot's depth texture: prepareSceneView looks it up every frame.
