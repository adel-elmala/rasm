Shadow mapping

A point is in shadow when something stands between it and the light. Shadow mapping answers that question with two passes: first draw the scene's depth as the light sees it, then, for each pixel the camera sees, compare its depth from the light with that stored depth. This page explains the idea, the math, the artifacts and their fixes. The last part shows how RASM does it.

In short

  • Pass 1 draws the scene from the light into a depth-only texture, the shadow map. Each texel keeps the depth of the surface nearest the light.
  • Pass 2 draws from the camera. For each pixel, it moves the surface point into the light's view and compares: farther than the stored depth means something is in front of it, so it is in shadow.
  • A directional light (the sun) uses an orthographic light camera; a spot light a perspective one; a point light six of them (a cube).
  • The map has a finite number of texels, so a surface can shadow itself: shadow acne. A small depth bias and a normal offset fix it.
  • One comparison per pixel gives hard, jagged edges. PCF averages several comparisons for a soft edge.

1The question: can the light see this point?

Lighting math uses the direction to the light and the surface normal. That lights every point that faces the light, even one behind a wall. A shadow is a different question: is the path from the point to the light free?

light in shadow

2The idea: depth from the light

Draw the scene from the light and keep only depth. Each texel of that image, the shadow map, now holds the distance to the first surface the light hits in that direction. A point farther away than that number has something in front of it.

the shadow map light rays lit in shadow

So shadow mapping is two passes over the same scene:

3The light's camera

The shadow pass needs a view and a projection, like any camera. The kind of light decides them.

Directional (sun)

Parallel rays from far away. An orthographic camera looking along the light. Its box must hold every object that can cast a shadow into the view.

1 map, or several cascades (section 7)

Spot

Rays from one point, inside a cone. A perspective camera at the light, with a field of view equal to the cone's angle.

1 map

Point

Rays from one point, in all directions. Six perspective cameras of 90° each, one per face of a cube around the light.

6 maps (a cube map)

For the orthographic camera, the size of the box is a design choice. The map has a fixed number of texels, and the box stretches them over the ground:

Too large

Each texel covers a lot of ground. Shadows are blocky and blurry.

Too small

Sharp shadows, but an object outside the box is not in the map, so it casts none.

Fitted

Just large enough for every caster and every receiver that the view can see.

The near plane counts too

The light camera's near plane must sit behind every caster. A caster between the light and the near plane is clipped away, and its shadow vanishes.

4The test, step by step

5Shadow acne, and the fixes

The map stores one depth per texel: the depth at the texel's center. A surface tilted toward the light changes depth inside one texel. Half of the surface under each texel is then "behind" the stored value and shadows itself, in stripes. That is shadow acne.

Four fixes, often combined:

Constant depth bias

Compare against the stored depth moved by a small fixed amount toward the fragment.

Too much: peter-panning (below)

Slope-scaled bias

A bias that grows with the surface's slope as the light sees it. The rasterizer can add it while drawing the map.

Too much: the same, on steep surfaces only

Normal offset

Look up the map from a point pushed off the surface along its normal: about one texel, more where the light grazes.

Too much: shadows shift sideways; thin objects leak light

Draw back faces only

The shadow pass culls front faces, so the map holds the far side of closed objects, away from the lit surface.

Fails for: open meshes and thin walls

Bias is a balance. Too little leaves acne; too much moves the shadow away from the object that casts it:

Too little: acne

Stripes on surfaces that should be lit.

Right

No stripes, and the shadow starts at the object's foot.

Too much: peter-panning

The shadow detaches, and the object seems to float.

Measure offsets in texels

Both offsets are best set in texels, not in fixed world units. Then they stay right when the map's size or the light camera's box changes.

6Soft edges: percentage-closer filtering

One comparison gives 0 or 1, so each shadow-map texel becomes a hard square on screen, and the shadow's edge is a staircase. Blurring the depths does not help: the average of two depths is a depth no surface has. Percentage-closer filtering (PCF) blurs the results instead: compare against several texels around the point, and average the 0s and 1s.

One comparison

Each pixel is 0 or 1. The edge is a staircase of map texels.

PCF: average of several

A pixel near the edge gets a value between 0 and 1: the share of its neighbours that are lit.

How the average is built decides how smooth the edge is. A common kernel reads a 4x4 block and weights it so that it acts as a 3x3 filter that slides with the point:

comparison says lit (1) comparison says shadow (0) the lookup point

A wider kernel gives a softer edge at more reads per pixel. Real penumbrae grow with the distance between the caster and the receiver; techniques like PCSS (percentage-closer soft shadows) estimate that distance first and widen the kernel to match.

7Resolution, coverage and cascades

A shadow-map texel covers box width / map size world units. Near the camera, one texel can cover many screen pixels: blocky shadows (perspective aliasing). Far away, many texels fall into one pixel and are wasted. Cascades fix this with several maps of the same size over boxes of different sizes.

Cascaded shadow maps (CSM)Split the camera's frustum by distance into 3 or 4 slices, each with its own map. The shader picks the cascade by the fragment's depth.Large outdoor scenes; the standard for a sun.

9Pitfalls

Stripes or moiré on lit surfaces
Shadow acne: no bias, or a bias smaller than half a texel's depth change.
Objects float; shadows start away from their feet
Too much bias (peter-panning).
A shadow is missing near the light
The caster is in front of the light camera's near plane, or outside its box.
Everything is in shadow, or nothing is
The comparison has the wrong direction (reversed-Z flips it), or v is flipped between the passes.
Shadow edges crawl when the camera moves
A box fitted to the view but not snapped to whole texels.
Blocky shadows close to the camera
One map stretched over too large an area: shrink the box, raise the resolution, or use cascades.
Reading the map gives garbage or validation errors
The depth texture was not moved from attachment to sampled between the passes, or was not created sampleable.

10In RASM

RASM shadows a directional light with cascaded shadow maps: up to four maps, each fitted every frame to a slice of the camera's view. Spot and point lights have shadows too (Spot and point light shadows), and a shaded view reads up to MAX_VIEW_SHADOWS (4). This section shows a directional light's. addShadowPasses adds a depth-only scene view per cascade, and addScenePasses adds the shaded view that reads them. All are ordinary render graph passes, so the graph orders them and adds every barrier. examples/06_shadows shows it, followed by a post pass, with no barrier in the example.

Keep these true
  • sampleShadow finds a light's maps only through its gfx::ShadowedLight (firstMap, mapCount); shadePBR multiplies only that light by it. A shadowed light is left out of the global and clustered loops, so it is shaded once.
  • The offsets take the texel size from the point's clip w, which is 1 in a cascade's orthographic box and the depth from the light in a perspective map, so one formula serves both.
  • The cascades' views and the shaded view must fit the cascades with the same inputs (camera, the shaded view's aspect ratio, light, SceneShadow), or the maps are read with other matrices than they were drawn with. addScenePasses only takes a shadow that addShadowPasses made for the same scene and camera in the same graph.
  • The test direction follows reversed-Z. Changing the depth convention changes it too (Projection and reversed-Z depth).
  • The scene passes must declare readDepth on every cascade, or they get no barrier and read one while it is still an attachment.
  • Each gfx::ShadowMap::texture holds the bindless index of this frame slot's depth texture of the map: prepareSceneView looks them up every frame.
