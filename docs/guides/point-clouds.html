Drawing

Point clouds

A laser scanner (LiDAR) or a photo survey gives millions of points: a position each, often a color or a value such as height or intensity. There are no triangles to draw. This page explains how to draw such a cloud fast: small splats on the screen, compact storage, a cheap level of detail, and colormaps that turn values into colors. The last part shows how RASM does it.

In short

  • Each point is a splat: a small square, disc or sphere on the screen, a few pixels or a few centimetres wide.
  • Points come in chunks of up to 65 536 near each other. A chunk stores each position as three 16-bit steps of its box: 8 bytes a point with a value, 12 with a color.
  • A point is one triangle around its square; the pixel stage cuts it to its shape.
  • Points of 2 pixels or less skip the triangle: a compute pass writes each pixel with a 64-bit max of depth and point, four (2 pixels) to ten (1 pixel) times faster per point, and a resolve puts them into the scene. Both paths take the same pixels.
  • The builder puts a chunk's points in an order where every prefix is an even sample. A chunk small on screen draws only its first points: level of detail with no extra data.
  • A colormap turns a value into a color through a range (linear or logarithmic) and a 256-entry texture.

1Splats: what a point looks like

A point has no size. To see it, the renderer draws a small shape at its place, a splat. Three shapes cover most uses.

Square

The cheapest. At 1 or 2 pixels every shape looks like this anyway.

Round

A disc. Its edge is smooth with several samples per pixel.

Sphere

A disc that writes a sphere's depth: overlapping points meet along a curve, as solid balls would.

The size is in pixels (the same on screen at every distance) or in metres (smaller when far away). A splat is never less than one pixel wide, so a far point neither vanishes nor flickers.

2One triangle a point

The usual splat is a quad: two triangles, four corners. But for points of one or two pixels the cost is per point (the corners and the triangle setup), not per pixel. One triangle that holds the square costs one setup instead of two; the pixel stage cuts it back to the square or the disc.

3Small points by compute

A triangle costs the same setup whether it covers 4 pixels or 400. For points of 1 or 2 pixels almost all the time goes there, not into the pixels. A compute shader can skip the triangle: one thread takes a point, finds the pixels its square covers, and writes them itself (Schütz, Kerbl and Wimmer, 2021).

The hard part is the depth test, which the triangle path gets for free. Many threads write the same pixel at once, in any order. The trick: put the depth and the point in one 64-bit word and keep the largest with a 64-bit atomic max (InterlockedMax in Slang). With reversed-Z a nearer point has a larger depth, so the max is the nearest point, whatever order the threads ran in.

Then a resolve draws a quad over the pixels the points can reach. Each pixel reads its word, finds the point, and writes its color, its depth and its motion into the scene's targets, with the normal depth test. So meshes in front of a point still hide it, and everything drawn later (labels, the grid, see-through draws, TAA) sees the points like any other surface.

Which chunk takes which path

The cull pass decides per chunk, after it chose the chunk's prefix. A chunk goes to compute when both hold:

s ≤ 2 pixels and k ≥ 0.125 × (its rect on screen, in pixels)

s
the points' size at the chunk's point nearest the camera: the largest they get
k
the points the chunk draws (its prefix)
rect
its box's corners on screen, and 2 pixels more: where its points can land

The first rule keeps the compute path to squares of at most 2 × 2 pixels; bigger points, and every round or sphere point big enough to show its shape, stay triangles. The second rule is the cost: the compute path pays per point a quarter (2 pixels) to a tenth (1 pixel) of a triangle, but once it runs it also clears and resolves every pixel of the rect its chunks cover. A sparse chunk spread over the screen is cheaper as triangles. A chunk the camera is inside of, or that is partly behind it, stays triangles too.

Why the two paths draw the same pixels

A chunk can switch paths from one frame to the next as the camera moves, so the two must agree to the pixel, or the switch would show. Both take a pixel when its centre lies in the point's square, edges included, and both place the square at the same centre: the compute path snaps it to 1/256 of a pixel, as the rasterizer snaps a triangle's corners. Under MSAA the compute path has one sample a pixel and the resolve writes it into all of the pixel's samples; in a view that also has the compute path, the triangle path covers whole pixels the same way, its triangle reaching half a pixel past the square so no sample of a covered pixel falls outside it. (A view drawn by triangles only keeps the smaller triangle, which is a fifth cheaper under MSAA; see Not yet.)

Filling the LOD's holes

The LOD keeps about as many points as the pixels a chunk covers, so a few pixels of a surface stay empty, and with whole pixels each shows as a single black dot (0.04 % of the top view of 28_point_cloud). The resolve fills them: an empty pixel takes the nearest of its 8 neighbours' points when at least 6 of them hold one, their depths are within 5 % of each other, and that point's chunk was thinned by the LOD.

4Storage: 16 bits in a chunk's box

Points near each other share a box. Inside it, a position needs far less than three 32-bit floats: 16 bits per axis is a step of 1/65 535 of the box, 0.5 mm for a 32 m box. A value (height, intensity, temperature) gets 16 bits too, in steps of the chunk's range of values.

8 Ba point with a valueagainst 16 B as floats
12 Ba point with a colorthe color as RGBA8, sRGB
0.5 mmstep in a 32 m box32 m / 65 535

The box also gives the chunk's bounding sphere, so a chunk is culled like any object: out of the view, or behind something in the depth of the frame before. The chunk is placed in the world by a transform in doubles, so a cloud far from the origin keeps its precision (large worlds).

5Level of detail by prefix

A chunk far away covers few pixels, and drawing all its points would put many on the same pixel. The cheap fix: draw only its first k points. That works only if the first k points are spread over the whole chunk, for every k. A scanner writes points line by line, and a random shuffle clumps. The builder sorts the points along a Morton curve (a path that visits every small square before the next one), then reads that list in bit-reversed steps.

  1. Quantize the positions to 16 bits in the box.
  2. Interleave the bits of x, y and z: the Morton code. Sort by it.
  3. For i = 0, 1, 2, … take sorted[reverse(i)], the bits of i reversed (0, P/2, P/4, 3P/4, …).
  4. The first 2j points are every P/2j-th point along the curve: one in each region.

The level of detail costs no memory: no second copy of the points, no tree. Its limit is that a chunk is one unit: a very large chunk near the camera draws all of its points even where part of it is far. Small chunks (tens of metres) keep that error small; a tree of chunks for whole datasets is the next step.

6How many points a chunk keeps

The chunk keeps enough points to cover its square on screen a few times. With D the diameter of its sphere on screen and a a point's area in pixels:

k = min(N, ⌈density × D2 / a⌉)

a = s2 (square), or π/4 × s2 (round, sphere)

D
pixels per unit at the sphere's nearest point × its diameter
s
the point's size in pixels, at least 1; a size in metres is converted at the same depth, so it cancels the distance
density
how many times the points should cover the chunk's square; 0 keeps them all

The π/4 matters: a disc covers less than its square. Without it, a density of 1 keeps as many points as 0.785 does with it, and leaves 1.3 % of the ground empty.

7Colormaps: values to colors

A value becomes a color in two steps: the range turns it into t from 0 to 1, and a 256 × 1 texture turns t into a color, read with linear filtering between its entries.

Perceptually even

viridis, magma, inferno, plasma and cividis change lightness evenly, so equal steps of the value look like equal steps. cividis also reads for color-blind viewers.

Turbo

A smooth rainbow: more distinct hues, good for heights, but its lightness goes up and down.

Gray

Lightness only, for intensity scans.

8Anti-aliasing and motion

MSAA: coverage from the distance

The shader turns the distance to the disc's edge into a coverage (alpha-to-coverage): half the samples of an edge pixel, a smooth rim. Points of 2 pixels or less have no rim to smooth: they cover whole pixels, all samples, on both paths, so a dense cloud shows no background through partly covered pixels.

TAA: the centre's motion

The projection's jitter moves the splat; every pixel of it writes the motion of the point's centre, so the history follows the point. The compute path projects with the same jittered matrix, and its resolve writes the same motion.

9Pitfalls

A far chunk is a stripe or a corner of itself
Its points are in scan order, so a prefix is a part. Build the chunk in a Morton order read in bit-reversed steps.
Holes in a surface at a distance
Too few points for the chunk's size on screen. Raise the density, or the point size.
Holes close to the camera
The data is that sparse: every point is drawn. Use a size in metres about the points' spacing.
A chunk changes look when it changes path
The compute path and the triangles take different pixels, or round a centre differently. Use one rule for both: a pixel whose centre is in the square, the centre snapped as the rasterizer snaps corners.
A small cloud costs more by compute
The clear and the resolve cost per pixel. Send a chunk to compute only when it draws enough points for the pixels it covers, and clear and resolve only that rect.
Round points vanish at 1 or 2 pixels
A disc that small misses the pixel centres it covers. Draw points under 3 pixels square.
Steps in the positions far from the origin
The points were floats in world space. Keep them as small offsets in their chunk, and the chunk's place in doubles.
Colors look washed out or too dark
The colormap's entries are sRGB; store them in an sRGB texture so the read gives linear color.

10In RASM

A chunk is a mesh made by buildPointCloudChunk, on any thread, and uploaded by Renderer::createMesh. An entity draws it with a MaterialType::POINTS material, whose PointMaterial gives the size, the shape, the colors and the density. The entity's transform places the chunk; culling, render space far from the origin and picking work as for any entity. Each view sends its small, dense chunks to the compute path where the GPU has 64-bit buffer atomics (shaderBufferInt64Atomics); RendererConfig::computePoints or Renderer::setComputePoints(false) draws every point by triangles.

Rules to keep

A chunk's order is part of its data: every prefix must stay an even sample. The cull pass decides each chunk's count in selectPointCount only, and its path in isRasterChunk only. The two paths must take the same pixels: a change to one's coverage, size or depth changes the other. PointData, PointChunkHeader and PointRasterHeader are kept in step on both sides (struct layout).

Not yet

  • Without the compute path (no 64-bit atomics, or computePoints off) a small point's triangle under MSAA leaves out the samples of a pixel past the square's left, bottom and diagonal sides (0.7 % of the pixels of the points_msaa4 test differ from the compute path's image), and nothing fills the LOD's holes: 0.04 % of the pixels of 28_point_cloud's top view stay black.
  • The hole fill is the compute path's only: a thinned chunk that switches to the triangles shows its few holes again (about 1 pixel in 2 500).
  • Streaming a cloud bigger than memory as a tree of chunks, and reading LAS files.
  • Points cast no shadows, and debug views draw them as they are (D50).
